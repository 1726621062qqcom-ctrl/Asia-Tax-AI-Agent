ABSTRACT
Legal systems are increasingly implemented through software, yet scalable meth-
ods for translating legal texts into accurate symbolic representations remain under-
developed. We study this problem through tax law, where forms and filing instruc-
tions define large computational structures involving arithmetic, branching, recur-
sion, and tabular reasoning. We propose LAW&ORDER, a neuro-symbolic frame-
work for automatically formalizing tax forms and instructions into executable
symbolic programs. Our approach establishes two forms of correspondence be-
tween law and logic: structural correspondence, which aligns legal and symbolic
components such as cells and schedules, and denotational correspondence, which
requires symbolic components to implement the computations specified by their
legal counterparts. We combine large language model synthesis with cell-level
verification and iterative localized error repair using human-written OpenTax-
Solver tax returns. We then evaluate the resulting formalizations on independently
authored, held-out TaxCalcBench returns, that are never exposed during genera-
tion or repair. Although the most advanced LLM achieves only 66% accuracy,
LAW&ORDER achieves 100% cell-level and form-level accuracy on 51 held-out
returns, demonstrating the effectiveness of combining LLM-based synthesis with
symbolic verification for scalable and verifiable large-scale legal autoformaliza-
tion compared with using an LLM alone.

INTRODUCTION
Modern societies increasingly rely on software to operationalize law (de la Garza, 2020; Lessig,
2009). Tax systems, regulatory frameworks, and other rule-based systems translate legal require-
ments written in natural language into executable procedures. Yet automating this translation re-
mains an open problem. Existing auto-formalization methods are concentrated primarily in mathe-
matics (Jiang et al., 2022; Zhou et al., 2024; Yang et al., 2026), while formalization in expert legal
domains remains largely manual, expensive, and difficult to scale (Lawsky, 2026b; Holzenberger
et al., 2020). For example, the IRS Direct File system required $16 million and more than two
years to build (Treasury Inspector General for Tax Administration, 2026). Beyond applications to
relatively small legal fragments (Judson et al., 2024; Lawsky, 2022), scalable automated legal for-
malization has yet to be realized.
Tax law provides a challenging setting for studying this problem. Existing implementations illustrate
its scale: OpenTaxSolver (Roberts, 2026) contains more than 16,000 lines of C code, while imple-
mentations of individual forms such as Form 1040 can exceed 1,000 lines. Moreover, legal rules
exist at different levels of abstraction. Statutes and other high-level legal sources are progressively
refined through regulations, guidance, forms, and instructions before ultimately being implemented
in software. We focus on IRS tax forms and their accompanying instructions. These documents
occupy a useful intermediate position between legal text and software. Unlike statutes and regula-
tions, they are written for ordinary taxpayers and express many legal requirements in comparatively
concrete and operational terms.
Tax forms also provide a natural structure for formalization and verification. A form consists of cells
containing numeric, Boolean, or finite-sort values. Some cells are taxpayer inputs, while others are
computed from preceding cells according to natural-language instructions. Formalization therefore

generated programs against expected cell-level outputs, and (3) an iterative repair procedure that
localizes discrepancies and revises the corresponding symbolic functions. The system therefore per-

formalization. Figure 1 illustrates this process.

correction. The final evaluation therefore measures whether formalizations developed using one set
of human-written cases generalize to independently written, previously unseen returns.

turally corresponding representations that align natural-language rules with executable units. Sec-
ond, we introduce a neuro-symbolic synthesis, verification, and repair framework that exploits this
structure for fine-grained error localization. Third, we demonstrate that resulting formalizations
generalize from human-written development cases to independently authored, CPA-written held-out
returns across 26 tax forms. Although the most advanced LLM can only achieve 66% accuracy,
LAW&ORDER achieves 100%, showing the effectiveness of our neuro-symbolic system.

RELATED WORK
2.1
AUTOFORMALIZATION METHODS AND VERIFICATION
Autoformalization has been studied primarily in mathematics, with benchmarks spanning Olympiad
problems, undergraduate theorems, Euclidean geometry, and large-scale Lean 4 corpora (Azerbayev
et al., 2023; Murphy et al., 2024; Gao et al., 2025; Li et al., 2024; Weng et al., 2025). Large language
models have shown substantial capability in translating natural-language mathematics into formal
specifications (Wu et al., 2022; Zheng et al., 2022; Azerbayev et al., 2023; Murphy et al., 2024),
while subsequent work has improved performance on larger formalization tasks through decompo-
sition, dependency information, and iterative correction (Liu et al., 2025; Wang et al., 2025; Min
et al., 2026). Evaluation, however, remains challenging: syntactic validity and successful compila-
tion do not guarantee that a generated formalization preserves the meaning of its source. Prior work
therefore supplements compilation with semantic evaluation or formal equivalence checks (Mur-
phy et al., 2024; Liu et al., 2025). This distinction is important for legal formalization, where a
well-formed executable program may encode a different rule from the underlying legal text.
2.2
COMPUTATIONAL LAW AND TAX-LAW FORMALIZATION
Computational-law research has long developed executable representations of legal rules. Catala
supports the systematic translation of statutory law into executable programs while preserving
default-and-exception structure (Merigoux et al., 2021), and related work frames tax-software con-
struction as legal formalization (Lawsky, 2022; 2026a); these approaches, however, largely rely
on human-authored formalizations. Legal benchmarks such as SARA and DeonticBench evaluate
statutory reasoning and executable Prolog generation (Holzenberger et al., 2020; Dou et al., 2026).
Formal methods have also been used for validation: CUTECat generates tests through concolic

METHOD
Our approach transforms tax forms and their accompanying instructions into an executable symbolic
representation assisted tax return examples. The pipeline consists of three stages: (1) form structure
extraction, (2) function signature generation, (3) instruction-grounded program synthesis and 4)
repair set-assisted program verification and correction.
The objective of our system is to synthesize a symbolic program that exactly reproduces the compu-
tations specified by a tax form and its accompanying instructions.
3.1
FORM STRUCTURE EXTRACTION
Given a tax form, we first use a regex-based algorithm for extracting all computationally relevant
cells and their associated definitions from the PDF file. Each cell and its definition corresponds to a
function in the resulting symbolic representation.
To evaluate the reliability of this stage, we manually audited the extracted representations across
a diverse collection of federal and state tax forms, including U.S. Form 1040 and its schedules,
as well as tax forms from Arizona, California, Massachusetts, Michigan, New Jersey, New York,
North Carolina, Ohio, Oregon, Pennsylvania, and Virginia. Across all audited forms, the extracted
cell structures matched the source documents without observed errors.
3.2
FUNCTION SIGNATURE GENERATION
For each extracted cell, we generate an empty function specification that represents the computation
required to determine the value of that cell. At this stage, only the function interface is generated;
the implementation logic is intentionally omitted. Let (P) denote the synthesized program and let
(T = t1, . . . , tn) denote a collection of benchmark tax returns from our repair set. Each repair set
example tj contains a complete set of input values and expected outputs. We evaluate the synthesized
functions by executing (F) on the repair set example inputs Ij and comparing the resulting cell values
against the repair set example outputs Gj. We then employ an iterative synthesis–verification loop
for symbolic logic generation.

Formally, each cell (ci : N|R|{true, false}) is associated with a function fi : Xi →Ci where
(Xi) denotes the set of input variables referenced by the tax form. The resulting collection of func-
tion signatures defines the computational skeleton of the tax form while remaining agnostic to the
underlying implementation language. The representation can be instantiated in any formal lan-
guage capable of expressing executable symbolic logic, including Epilogue, C++, Lean, Python, or
domain-specific languages.
As with the extraction stage, we manually verified the correctness of generated function signatures
across all audited tax forms and observed no errors.
3.3
INSTRUCTION-GROUNDED PROGRAM SYNTHESIS
Tax forms are accompanied by official instructions that specify how each cell should be computed 1.
We leverage these instructions as the authoritative semantic source for program generation as they
are specifically drafted for ordinary tax-payers, logically well-structured, and require substantially
less legal interpretation than statues and Code of Federal Regulations.
During this step, an LLM receives (1) the tax form, (2) the corresponding instruction text, and
(3) the function signatures. The model synthesizes executable symbolic logic for each function
signature that implements the computation described in the instructions. Conceptually, this stage
performs a translation from natural-language legal rules into formal executable specifications. The
synthesized logic may use any formal language that preserves the tax instructions’ semantics and
supports deterministic execution.
3.4
REPAIR SET-ASSISTED VERIFICATION AND CORRECTION
Recent work has increasingly emphasized that syntactic validity alone is insufficient for evaluating
autoformalization. Liu et al. (2025) introduce an equivalence-based metric for measuring faithful
formalization. We introduce two complementary forms of correspondence for law and software.
Structural correspondence asks whether components of the legal artifact—such as cells, schedules,
and dependencies—have corresponding components in the symbolic representation. Denotational
correspondence asks whether those components produce the prescribed behavior. Tax forms provide
an unusually useful setting for the latter because benchmark returns expose intermediate cell values.
Discrepancies can therefore be localized to individual symbolic functions and used to drive targeted
repair. Agreement on a finite benchmark establishes cell-level behavioral agreement on the repair
set examples rather than universal semantic equivalence over all possible taxpayer inputs.
To validate the synthesized program, we compare its outputs—specifically, the cell values computed
by each generated function—against an independently generated repair set of tax returns. Each ex-
ample in the repair set consists of a set of input variables together with the expected values for all
cells in the form. Validation proceeds sequentially, comparing cell values step by step. When a
discrepancy is identified, the function corresponding to the affected cell is regenerated by prompting
the LLM with the relevant tax form and instruction context to correct the implementation. The vali-
dation process is then repeated. This iterative refinement cycle continues until the outputs produced
through symbolic execution match the repair set examples for every cell in the form.
Because tax forms define a deterministic computation graph, agreement at every intermediate cell
implies agreement for the repair-set examples on the final tax outputs. Consequently, the final syn-
thesized program is accepted only when it reproduces the complete computation trace without any
inconsistencies.

Baselines
We compare five approaches to tax-return computation. Without formalization directly
computes tax returns from taxpayer inputs and tax instructions without constructing an executable
symbolic representation. Without formalization + Web Search (WS) follows the same approach but
additionally allows the model to retrieve relevant information from the Web. Direct translation (Han

program using execution feedback. Finally, LAW&ORDER performs targeted program repair using
localized execution feedback.

4.3
MAIN RESULTS
Table 1 shows that LAW&ORDER ’s primary strength is its ability to substantially improve weaker
initial formalizations through localized execution feedback, with the clearest gains appearing for
GPT-5.5 (Medium). For this model, LAW&ORDER increases strict return-level accuracy from only
17.5% under direct translation and 37.5% under self-debugging to 95.0%, while increasing cell-level
accuracy from 73.3% and 75.0% to 99.3%, respectively. This large gap over self-debugging suggests
that localized feedback can be particularly effective when global debugging fails to identify or isolate
errors. LAW&ORDER also reaches 100% return- and cell-level accuracy with GPT-6 and substan-
tially improves over direct translation for several other models. However, LAW&ORDER does not
consistently outperform conventional self-debugging: Claude Opus 5.5, Claude Fable 5.1, and Kimi
K3 perform better with self-debugging, while the two approaches are comparable for Gemini 3.8
Flash. We hypothesize that this difference may arise because Claude models have been optimized
for global self-debugging and agentic coding workflows, making them particularly effective at re-
pairing programs from execution feedback without explicit localization. Finally, LAW&ORDER
cannot fully compensate for insufficient underlying model capability, as Qwen 3.6 8B remains at 0%
return-level accuracy despite modest improvements at the cell level. Overall, these results suggest
that LAW&ORDER is especially valuable when global self-debugging is ineffective—as illustrated
by the large improvement for GPT-5.5—although its advantage is model-dependent.
Importantly, the results of self-debugging and LAW&ORDER should not be interpreted as the
accuracy of a single LLM generation pass.
Rather, they reflect the accuracy of the complete
synthesis–verification-repair system, which combines program generation, symbolic execution, dis-
crepancy localization, and iterative correction until consistency is achieved. We also show the results
of model performance with formalization on Tax Year 2024 in Table 2. GPT-5.5 performs relatively
poorly with LAW&ORDER on Tax Year 2024 primarily because it fails to correctly formalize Form
1040 Line 35a, which computes the tax refund. This cell is relatively complex, involving four oper-
ations, including a division. Because Line 35a lies on the main computation path, this error affects
all returns that result in a refund, substantially reducing return-level accuracy. Returns without a
refund are unaffected by this error.
Overall, these results demonstrate the value of explicit symbolic structure in domains such as taxa-
tion and law, where correctness depends on adherence to formal rules rather than linguistic plausi-
bility alone. Combining natural-language understanding with executable symbolic representations
enables intermediate computations to be inspected, verified, and repaired. By grounding computa-
tion in explicit symbolic structures, LAW&ORDER facilitates fine-grained verification and localized
correction of generated formalizations.

4.4
PROVING SEMANTIC EQUIVALENCE AND DIVERGENCE
We uniformly sampled 50 cells and compare the implementations generated by Claude Opus 5.5
with their corresponding gold implementations. We use Claude Code to construct formal proofs of
equivalence or, when the implementations differ semantically, to identify inputs that demonstrate
their divergence. For 43 of the 50 sampled cells, equivalence is successfully proven. For the re-
maining seven cells, Claude Code identifies counterexamples under which the generated and gold
implementations produce different outputs. Manual inspection shows that six of these divergences
result from errors in the Opus 5.5 implementations, while one reveals an error in the gold imple-
mentation: it assigns a $400,000 benefit to qualifying widowed taxpayers rather than the $200,000
specified in the tax instructions. Examples of equivalence proofs and counterexamples are provided
in Appendix A.1.
5
ANALYSIS
5.1
COMPLEXITY ANALYSIS OF THE TEST SET.
Figure 4 shows the distribution of reasoning depth across the 1,847 cells in the 26 benchmark forms.
While 1,021 cells correspond to raw inputs at depth 0, the remaining cells span a broad range of
reasoning depths, reflecting computations that depend on increasingly long chains of intermediate
values. A substantial number of cells require multiple levels of reasoning, with depths of 5, 10,
and beyond representing nontrivial dependency chains. The distribution extends to a maximum
depth of 30, illustrating that tax-form computation can involve deeply nested dependencies and long
sequences of interconnected calculations.
5.2
ACCURACY VS LENGTH OF CALCULATION CHAINS
From Figure 3, we show that errors are concentrated among examples with longer calculation chains,
indicating that model accuracy decreases as reasoning depth increases. This pattern is consistent
with the hypothesis that intermediate mistakes accumulate over multi-step computations, leading to
a higher probability of failure on long reasoning trajectories. Similar phenomena have been reported
in prior deductive reasoning and math reasoning benchmarks (Han et al., 2024b; Miner et al., 2025)
where model performance degrades as the length and complexity of the reasoning chain increase.
As shown in Figure 5 and Figure 6, in both repair and test sets, LAW&ORDER shows a more sta-
ble iterative improvement than self-debugging. On the repair set, trace-level accuracy generally
increases steadily under LAW&ORDER, and these gains are accompanied by rapid improvements
in line-level accuracy on the independently authored test set, with GPT-6 reaching 100% and GPT-
5.5 reaching 99.8%. In contrast, although self-debugging performs well with the stronger models,
it is highly unstable with GPT-5.5, exhibiting substantial regressions and fluctuations on both sets.
These results indicate that LAW&ORDER provides a more consistent optimization trajectory and
that improvements on the repair set generally transfer to the held-out test set.

5.3
LINE COVERAGE OF THE TEST CASES
Tax forms exhibit relatively limited branching structure, allowing most cells to be exercised with
only one or two examples. For Tax Year (TY) 2024, the repair set, containing at most two inputs
per form, achieves 94.6% cell coverage and 44.0% branch coverage. By comparison, the 51-input
test set increases coverage to 98.6% of cells and 73.1% of branches. A similar pattern holds for
TY 2025: the repair set achieves 93.3% cell coverage and 44.0% branch coverage, whereas the 51-
input test set achieves 93% cell coverage and 73% branch coverage. Thus, even a small number
of examples exercises the vast majority of tax-form cells, although substantially more examples are
required to cover alternative execution paths. Despite the sparsity of the repair sets, the relatively
rigid structure and unambiguous wording of tax forms allow limited execution feedback to provide
broad coverage of the underlying computations.

CONCLUSION
We proposed LAW&ORDER, an iterative optimization framework for large-scale tax law auto-
formalization with localized program repair. Extensive experiments and analyses demonstrate that
LAW&ORDER achieves strong performance beyond the human-written repair cases used for op-
timization, including on independently authored, CPA-written held-out tax returns across 30 tax
forms. On these held-out returns, a frontier LLM correctly computes only 66% of returns, whereas
LAW&ORDER achieves 100% accuracy. Our analysis further shows that iterative repair consistently

A.1
DETAILS ON TAX FILING AND TAX FORMS.
Federal income tax filing.
The federal income tax filing process can be characterized as a se-
quence of interdependent computations that transform reported income into a final tax settlement.
Taxpayers first aggregate income from sources such as wages, interest, dividends, retirement distri-
butions, and Social Security benefits, after which eligible adjustments are applied to derive Adjusted
Gross Income (AGI). AGI constitutes a central intermediate quantity, as numerous subsequent eligi-
bility criteria, phase-outs, and tax computations depend on it.
Taxable income is subsequently derived from AGI by applying the relevant deductions, including the
standard or itemized deduction and, where applicable, the Qualified Business Income (QBI) deduc-
tion. The resulting taxable income forms the basis for computing tax liability under the applicable
IRS tax tables and worksheets, with separate computational rules governing income that receives
preferential tax treatment, such as qualified dividends and capital gains. Finally, applicable tax cred-
its and prior payments, including withholding and estimated tax payments, are applied against the
resulting liability. This reconciliation determines the taxpayer’s final position as either a refund or a
balance due.
Progressively refined legal rules.
As illustrated in Figure 7, statutes and other high-level legal
sources are progressively refined through regulations, guidance, forms, and instructions before ulti-
mately being implemented in software.
