LLM-assisted formalization for deterministic detection 
of statutory inconsistency in tax law
Borchuluun Yadamsuren1 · Steven Keith Platt1 · Miguel Diaz1
Abstract
This study introduces a hybrid neuro-symbolic framework that achieves determin­
istic detection of statutory inconsistency in complex law. We use the U.S. Internal 
Revenue Code (IRC) for our investigation because its complexity makes it a fertile 
domain for identifying conflicts. Our research advances a solution for detecting 
inconsistent provisions by combining Large Language Models (LLMs) with a sym­
bolic reasoner.
To evaluate this approach, we conducted experiments using GPT-4o, GPT-5, and 
Prolog. First, we used GPT-4o to translate IRC Section   121 into formal Prolog 
rules, which were then refined in the SWISH environment. Next, these rules were 
incorporated into GPT-4o prompts to test whether Prolog-augmention would im­
prove the ability to detect legal inconsistencies.
The results show that Prolog augmentation did not improve the model’s success 
rate. Whether relying on natural language alone or using the Prolog-augmented 
prompts, GPT-4o only detected the inconsistency in one out of three testing strate­
gies, resulting in a low 33% accuracy rate. Furthermore, the model’s underlying 
reasoning was actually worse when using the augmented prompts: the natural-
language approach achieved 100% rule coverage, whereas the Prolog-augmented 
approach only reached 66%, indicating that the model missed critical steps in its 
statutory analysis.
In contrast to probabilistic prompting, the hybrid Prolog model produced de­
terministic and reproducible results. Guided by GPT-5 for refinement, the model 
formalized the IRC section’s competing interpretations and successfully detected an 
inconsistency zone. Validation tests confirm that the Prolog implementation is ac­
curate, internally consistent, deterministic, and capable of autonomously identifying 
inconsistencies. These findings show that LLM-assisted formalization, anchored in 
symbolic logic, enables transparent and reliable statutory inconsistency detection.
Keywords  Legal AI · Tax Law · Statutory Reasoning · Large Language Models · 
Prolog · Symbolic Logic · Inconsistency Detection

1  Introduction
AI is being deployed in many fields, including in the legal domain. For example, 
Bernsohn et al. (2024) leveraged LLMs to identify legal violations in unstructured 
text, demonstrating the potential for LLMs to handle complex legal classification 
tasks. Similarly, Bui et al. (2024) utilized LLM-based data augmentation techniques 
to address data scarcity in legal datasets, significantly improving entailment and 
retrieval tasks. Despite these advances, LLMs often struggle with precise and con­
sistent legal reasoning due to their probabilistic nature and inability to operate on 
explicit logical structures. This has led to increasing interest in neuro-symbolic sys­
tems that combine LLMs with deterministic reasoning tools. Numerous studies have 
explored using Prolog, a widely adopted logic programming language (Körner et al., 
2022), to complement LLMs in addressing these reasoning limitations.1 For example, 
Sehgal and Liu (2025) developed the LogicLease system for analyzing landlord-ten­
ant legal cases by incorporating a Prolog component. Zin et al. (2025) investigated 
prompting strategies to formalize traffic rules in Prolog using LLMs.
Notwithstanding, the use of LLMs to assist in tax-related investigations is particu­
larly challenging. Oei and Osofsky (2018) note, for example, that U.S. tax laws are 
complex, often ambiguous, and not particularly intuitive. This body of law is also 
characterized by highly technical language and intricate cross-references. In addi­
tion, it is also complicated and ever-evolving. For instance, Oei and Osofsky (2018) 
conducted in-depth interviews with government attorneys who participated in the tax 
legislative process over four decades. They identified several compounding issues, 
such as the accretion of statutory language over time, limitations on changing exist­
ing statutory language, time pressure while drafting tax legislation, low likelihood 
of technical corrections, and a lack of prioritization to clean up the tax code. Legal 
logic scholars have further observed that in complex statutory systems, inconsisten­
cies may arise not only from drafting errors but also from the structural interaction of 
legal norms across provisions (Araszkiewicz and Płeszka 2015).
Lawsky (2026) has also recognized the complexity of tax law drafting by point­
ing out an inconsistency and contradiction in the tax code. Extending this work, we 
examine whether AI-assisted formalization can support systematic inconsistency 
detection. This study addresses two related questions. First, can GPT-4o, when 
prompted with natural language and Prolog-augmented prompts, reliably detect a 
known inconsistency in IRC § 121? Second, can a hybrid approach, in which an 
LLM assists in generating and refining formal Prolog representations of statutory 
text, produce a deterministic inconsistency detection system that reliably identifies 
conflicts across a diverse set of use cases? In this design, LLMs play two roles. In 
RQ1, GPT-4o is evaluated by comparing natural-language prompting with prompts 
augmented by Prolog rules generated by the model to determine whether prompt aug­
mentation improves inconsistency detection. These Prolog rules were subsequently

2  Related work
This section reviews prior research relevant to computational approaches to statutory 
reasoning. First, it examines studies evaluating the capabilities and limitations of 
Large Language Models in legal reasoning tasks.
Second, it discusses emerging hybrid approaches that combine natural language 
processing with symbolic reasoning systems for statutory analysis.
2.1  Large Language Models in legal reasoning
Recent research has explored LLM capabilities in legal contexts with mixed results. 
Nay et al. (2024) tested various LLMs on multiple-choice tax law questions. They 
found that while giving models additional legal context slightly increased accuracy, 
Chain of Thought (CoT) prompting did not consistently improve performance across 
models. They concluded that the models fell short of expert-level performance in tax 
law applications.
Large-scale benchmark studies have produced similar findings. Fei et al. (2024) 
introduced the LawBench framework to evaluate LLM performance across three 
cognitive levels: legal knowledge memorization, understanding, and application. 
Their extensive evaluation of fifty-two LLMs found GPT-4o to be the best-perform­
ing model in legal domains, though significant limitations remained in complex rea­
soning tasks due to the inherent limitations of generative AI models in handling the 
intricate cognitive tasks required for legal reasoning. Similarly, Blair-Stanek et al. 
(2023) observed frequent failures in GPT-3’s statutory reasoning due to incomplete 
or inaccurate prior knowledge of statutory content and inherent reasoning limitations. 
Guha et al. (2023) developed a legal reasoning benchmark based on the empirical 
evaluation of 20 open-source and commercial LLMs by testing 162 different tasks 
covering six types of legal reasoning. They found significant variation in the perfor­
mance of LLMs across tasks.
Kant et al. (2025) emphasized that recent LLMs such as OpenAI o1 have dem­
onstrated notable improvements in legal reasoning, with newer models like Deep­
Seek R1 and OpenAI o3-mini achieving comparable results at lower computational 
cost. These developments suggest that reasoning-oriented LLMs hold the promise for 
automating complex legal tasks.
These findings suggest that purely probabilistic models may struggle with legal 
reasoning tasks that require precise logical interpretation of statutory rules.
2.2  Hybrid approaches combining LLMs and symbolic reasoning
Recognizing these limitations, recent research has explored hybrid approaches that 
combine natural language processing with symbolic reasoning systems. Holzenberger 
et al. (2020) established foundational research in computational statutory reasoning 
by developing the Statutory Reasoning Assessment (SARA) dataset, focusing explic­
itly on U.S. law. Their work highlighted a clear distinction between probabilistic 
approaches using LLMs such as BERT and Legal BERT, and deterministic symbolic 
methods using the Prolog language. While probabilistic models showed flexibility,

Further, Holzenberger and Durme (2023) refined this approach by proposing

to reduce the substantial human effort previously needed for the manual translation of

such as LEGALBERT, could significantly improve downstream symbolic reason­
ing accuracy. However, automated IE still encountered challenges, such as missing 
critical elements, including monetary values or specific dates, leading to diminished 
reasoning accuracy.
More recently, Kant et al. (2024) emphasized the importance of differentiating 
deterministic and non-deterministic components of legal reasoning before applying 
AI. They successfully translated laws and insurance contracts into Prolog rules using 
LLMs, finding that OpenAI’s o1-preview model significantly outperformed GPT-4o 
in generating accurate Prolog queries. In addition, Kant et al. (2025) evaluated the 
effectiveness of combining LLMs with Prolog-based logic encoding by comparing 
three approaches: direct (vanilla) LLM prompting, unguided Prolog generation, and 
expert-guided Prolog generation based on a predefined and validated set of insur­
ance claim coverage questions and answers from two U.S. health insurance policies. 
Under the expert-guided approach, the GPT-4o and o1 models performed with 100% 
accuracy for the simplified version of the insurance contract. For the more complex 
insurance policy, the OpenAI o1 model demonstrated higher accuracy than other 
models. The Prolog code was evaluated using the SWISH interpreter, highlighting 
the value of symbolic logic for generating interpretable and auditable legal outputs.
Together, this research suggests that combining LLMs with symbolic reason­
ing systems can improve the reliability and transparency of computational legal 
reasoning.

3.1  Complexity of the Internal Revenue Code
The Internal Revenue Code is notoriously complex (Choi 2020). Taxpayers face 
challenges both in interpreting and combining disparate provisions of the IRC. Choi 
(2020) highlights how judicial application of anti-abuse doctrines, for example, cre­
ates additional inconsistency and confusion, particularly when substance-over-form 
doctrines conflict with provisions that are purely formal in nature.
Oei and Osofsky (2018) document how Congress has extensively modified tax 
legislation over the years, with the speed of passing new legislation drawing protests 
from experts due to the resulting complexity. They advocate for a complete paradigm 
shift in the legal drafting process and specifically highlight the importance of using 
modern technology, including artificial intelligence, to optimize tax law drafting and 
analysis.
3.2  Framework for legal inconsistency
Legal inconsistency has been examined from multiple perspectives in legal theory 
and computational legal studies. Donelson (2019) provides a detailed analysis of 
legal inconsistency and distinguishes several forms, including irreconcilable incon­
sistencies between laws, reconcilable inconsistencies, inconsistencies between legal 
justifications, and inconsistencies between justification and execution. In this frame­
work, an inconsistency becomes irreconcilable when accepted methods of legal 
interpretation, such as harmonization, prioritization, or purposive interpretation, can­
not resolve the conflict between provisions. This perspective highlights the point at 
which inconsistency becomes visible when legal rules are applied in practice.
Other scholars argue that legal consequences cannot be understood simply as 
logical consequences derived mechanically from statutory text. Araszkiewicz and 
Płeszka (2015) describe legal consequences as normative consequences, attributed 
through interpretive and argumentative practices. As a result, some deductively valid 
inferences may be rejected in legal reasoning, while other outcomes may be accepted 
despite lacking a strictly logical derivation.
Legal logic research approaches the issue by examining how conflicts arise within 
formal representations of normative systems. Lindahl and Reidhav (2015) define nor­
mative conflict as a situation in which a set of norms entails incompatible outcomes 
under the same factual conditions. They distinguish between contradictions, where 
incompatible normative statuses are entailed, and compliance conflicts, where obli­
gations or consequences cannot be jointly satisfied.
Recent work in computational legal studies has further examined how problem­
atic outcomes may arise when statutory rules are represented and executed as logic 
programs. Fungwacharakorn et al. (2021) investigate situations in which a literal 
computational interpretation of statutes produces counterintuitive legal consequences 
or outcomes that conflict with commonly accepted legal reasoning or social expecta­
tions. Their approach models statutes as logic programs and uses a debugging-style 
procedure to identify a “culprit,” defined as the rule or rule interaction responsible 
for producing such unintended results. This work illustrates how formal representa­
tions of statutes can make hidden conflicts or problematic rule interactions computa­

The present study identifies a statutory inconsistency that emerges when § 121 is 
applied to specific fact patterns, producing irreconcilably different legal outcomes 
depending on how the interacting provisions are applied. The specific section of the 
Internal Revenue Code analyzed is § 121, which governs the exclusion of gain from 
the sale of a taxpayer’s principal residence. The relevant statutory language provides:
“Gross income shall not include gain from the sale or exchange of property if, 
during the 5-year period ending on the date of the sale or exchange, such property 
has been owned and used by the taxpayer as the taxpayer’s principal residence for 
periods aggregating 2 years or more.” (26 U.S.C. § 121(a)).
“The amount of gain excluded from gross income under subsection (a) with 
respect to any sale or exchange shall not exceed $250,000.” (26 U.S.C. § 121(b)(1)).
The exclusion ceiling doubles from $250,000 to $500,000 for a husband and wife 
who make a joint return for the taxable year of the sale or exchange, provided all 
three of the following conditions are satisfied:
“Paragraph (1) shall be applied by substituting “$500,000” for “$250,000” if-
(i)	
either spouse meets the ownership requirements of subsection (a) with respect 
to such property;
(ii)	 both spouses meet the use requirements of subsection (a) with respect to such 
property; and.
(iii)	 neither spouse is ineligible for the benefits of subsection (a) with respect to such 
property by reason of paragraph (3).” (26 U.S.C. § 121(b)(2)(A)).
Where the conditions of § 121(b)(2)(A) are not fully satisfied, § 121(b)(2)(B) gov­
erns instead: the couple’s exclusion ceiling becomes the aggregate of the individual 
limitations to which each spouse would be entitled as an unmarried taxpayer.
Finally, Section 121(c) provides a partial exclusion for taxpayers who fail to sat­
isfy the ownership and use requirements of § 121(a), or who are otherwise barred by 
the two-year sale frequency limitation of § 121(b)(3), provided the sale or exchange 
is driven by a qualifying reason, a change in place of employment, health, or unfore­
seen circumstances. Rather than denying relief entirely, § 121(c) scales the exclusion 
proportionally to the taxpayer’s actual period of qualifying residence. It operates as 
an independent waiver provision: when triggered, it suspends both the ownership 
and use requirements of § 121(a) and the frequency bar of § 121(b)(3), substituting a 
reduced cap in place of the full limitation. The provision thus reflects, in the authors’ 
reading, a legislative judgment that hardship-driven sales warrant partial, rather than 
categorical, exclusion.
Section 121(c)(1) specifies the base against which the proportional reduction is 
calculated:
“the dollar limitation under paragraph (1) or (2) of subsection (b), whichever 
is applicable.” (§ 121(c)(1)).

Section 121(c)(1) directs that the proportional reduction applies to “the dollar limita­
tion under paragraph (1) or (2) of subsection (b), whichever is applicable.” It is this 
phrase, “whichever is applicable,” that generates an interpretive difficulty for married 
couples who fall outside § 121(b)(2)(A) but retain some exclusion entitlement under 
§ 121(b)(2)(B). The statute does not specify which subsection (b) limitation governs 
when § 121(c) is applied to such couples: the $500,000 joint cap, or the aggregate 
of individual caps computed under § 121(b)(2)(B). Because the two bases produce 
different exclusion amounts, the choice of referent is outcome-determinative. Impor­
tantly, this situation does not arise from a discretionary choice by the taxpayer among 
alternative rules. Rather, the statutory language permits two competing interpreta­
tions that can both be derived from the text itself. When applied to identical factual 
conditions, these interpretations yield incompatible legal outcomes, which consti­
tutes the form of statutory inconsistency examined in this study. This structural gap 
exemplifies what Lawsky (2026) terms a condition failure, a breakdown that occurs 
when interacting provisions of a statute fail to yield a determinate result because the 
conditions triggering each provision were not drafted with their interaction in mind.
Applying Donelson’s definition of irreconcilable inconsistency, Lawsky identi­
fied this as a case that yields inconsistent outcomes under the same set of facts. This 
inconsistency arises in the specific case where a married couple filing jointly fails to 
satisfy § 121(b)(2)(A) because one spouse cannot independently meet the two-year 
use requirement, yet retains some exclusion entitlement under § 121(b)(2)(B), while 
also triggering § 121(c)’s partial exclusion relief. The statute directs taxpayers to 
apply the proportional reduction to ‘the dollar limitation under paragraph (1) or (2) of 
subsection (b), whichever is applicable,’ but provides no mechanism for determining 
which limitation is ‘applicable’ when the spouses’ individual qualifications diverge.
This creates scenarios where the same facts can yield different legal outcomes, 
depending on interpretation, which is a classic example of irreconcilable inconsisten­
cy.2 Accordingly, this study defines a statutory inconsistency as occurring when alter­
native interpretations of the same statutory provisions generate incompatible legal 
outcomes under identical factual conditions. We formalize these competing interpre­
tations as Prolog rules and evaluate whether Large Language Models can detect the 
resulting inconsistency.

3.3  Formalization of tax code
Early research in artificial intelligence and law explored the use of logic program­
ming to formalize tax legislation and automate tax-related reasoning. Sherman (1987) 
developed one of the earliest Prolog-based computational models of tax statutes by 
encoding portions of the Canadian Income Tax Act as logical rules capable of analyz­
ing the legal consequences of transactions.
In this system, legal facts were represented as Prolog predicates describing tax­
payers, assets, and transactions. From these input facts, Prolog performs an initial 
stage of forward-chaining inference, deriving relationships such as taxpayer status 
and interdependencies among taxpayers and assets. Once these derived relationships 
are established, the system can answer specific legal questions by backward-chaining 
over the rule base, evaluating whether a particular legal consequence follows from 
the available facts (Sherman 1987).
In total, the model included 26 factual predicates describing entities such as tax­
payers, assets, shareholdings, and transactions. Examples include:

ration;

time. Additional predicates encode information such as share ownership, residency 
status, and taxpayer death events, enabling the system to model complex tax sce­
narios involving multiple entities and transactions. This representation demonstrates 
how statutory conditions and transactional facts can be translated into executable log­
ical structures. By combining structured factual predicates with rule-based inference, 
Prolog enables statutory provisions to be evaluated across complex fact patterns in a 
transparent and reproducible manner.
Subsequent work explored the integration of logic programming with practi­
cal tax software. Chang (1997) developed CyberTax™, a system that combined a 
C/C + + application with a Prolog rule base to automate the completion of U.S. tax 
forms. In this architecture, Prolog encoded the logical relationships among form 
fields and tax rules, allowing the system to automatically propagate values across 
related forms and recalculate dependent values through Prolog’s backtracking and 
rule evaluation mechanisms. This approach demonstrated the advantages of declara­
tive rule representations for modeling tax regulations, particularly because tax rules 
often consist of structured relationships among data elements that change over time.

Another line of research has focused on developing specialized programming 
languages and formal logic frameworks that more closely mirror the hierarchical 
structure of legal rules. Lawsky (2017) advanced the idea that translating statutory 
language into formal logical representations could help prevent unintentional ambi­
guity and make legal rules computationally interpretable. Further, Lawsky (2018) 
argues that statutory reasoning is defeasible, meaning that conclusions drawn from 
general rules may be overridden by more specific provisions. To represent this struc­
ture, she proposes modeling statutes using default logic, a form of non-monotonic 
logic in which rules operate with explicit priorities reflecting the hierarchical organi­
zation of statutory provisions.
Merigoux et al. (2021) developed Catala, a domain-specific programming lan­
guage designed to formalize statutory rules while preserving their hierarchical struc­
ture. Catala operationalizes the idea that legal rules can be represented as prioritized 
rules with exceptions, allowing statutes to be translated into executable programs that 
mirror legislative drafting patterns. The authors demonstrate this approach by formal­
izing provisions of the Internal Revenue Code, including § 121 on the exclusion of 
gain from the sale of a principal residence, showing how complex statutory rules can 
be represented as computational logic.
Despite these advances, translating statutory provisions into executable logic 
remains labor-intensive. Statutory texts are complex and rely heavily on patterns 
of general rules followed by exceptions, which makes their faithful computational 
representation challenging (Mérigoux et al., 2021). Moreover, encoding statutes in 
Catala typically requires a form of pair programming in which legal experts interpret 
the statute while programmers translate those interpretations into executable logic 
(Lawsky 2022). Although this collaborative process helps ensure that the computa­
tional model accurately reflects the structure and intent of the law, it also creates a 
significant practical challenge because it requires expertise in both legal interpreta­
tion and programming.

Earlier logic-programming systems such as those developed by Sherman (1987) 
and Chang (1997) demonstrated the feasibility of representing tax law as executable 
logical rules and highlighted the potential of logic programming for legal reasoning 
and tax computation. However, these approaches primarily focused on rule execution 
and tax calculation rather than on detecting inconsistencies or ambiguities within 
statutory language itself.
The present study builds on this foundation by combining large language models 
with symbolic reasoning tools to formalize tax statutes and identify potential incon­
sistencies within the Internal Revenue Code.
3.4  Computational approaches to statutory inconsistency detection
Lawsky (2026) developed a Python- and Catala-based pipeline for detecting incon­
sistency in IRC § 121. In this work, two alternative formalizations of the statute are 
implemented: a per-taxpayer “sum of limitations” reading that mirrors IRS Publica­
tion 523,3 and a joint-cap reading that scales the $500,000 limit by a joint numerator. 
Using Python with the Z3 theorem prover (de Moura and Bjørner 2008), she encodes 
the statute’s temporal tests and dollar limits, then systematically explores ownership, 
use, prior exclusions, and gain to identify fact patterns where the two readings pro­
duce different results. Lawsky argues that inconsistencies in statutes do not operate 
like contradictions in formal logic; rather, they are typically resolved by legal institu­
tions such as courts, administrative agencies, or legislatures. Accordingly, program­
ming languages designed to encode legal rules should restrict their expressiveness 
so that unresolved statutory inconsistencies cannot be directly implemented. Catala 
reflects this design principle by relying on prioritized default logic, which requires 
potentially conflicting rules to be ordered in advance and forces the programmer to 
make rule priority explicit before execution (Lawsky 2022; Mérigoux et al. 2021). In 
contrast, Z3 reveals scenarios where different interpretations of the statute lead to dif­
ferent outcomes, exposing ambiguities in the statutory rules. Together, these methods 
move formalization from concept to practice by enabling operational inconsistency 
checking, with open code that makes the modeling choices transparent and reproduc­
ible (Lawsky, 2026; Lawsky, n.d.).
Recent research has explored automated detection of inconsistencies in rule-based 
textual systems outside statutory law. Zhang et al. (2021) formulate the contract incon­
sistency checking problem as a pairwise classification task, where the goal is to deter­

4  Research questions
4.1  Research gap and contributions
Despite recent advances in computational legal studies, research on using large lan­
guage models to detect structural inconsistencies within complex statutory frame­
works such as tax law remains limited. Existing research on legal reasoning with 
LLMs has largely focused on tasks such as legal question answering, document anal­
ysis, and contract review. However, the ability of LLMs to identify inconsistencies 
that arise from the interaction of multiple statutory provisions remains underexplored.
This study addresses this gap through a two-part investigation. First, we evaluate 
whether GPT-4o can detect a known statutory inconsistency in IRC § 121 through 
prompting, using both natural language prompts and prompts augmented with Pro­
log-based reasoning structures. Second, we examine whether a hybrid approach, in 
which an LLM assists in generating and refining Prolog representations of statutory 
rules, can support a deterministic symbolic reasoning workflow capable of identify­
ing statutory inconsistencies across multiple factual scenarios.
This research makes four distinct contributions to the AI legal literature:
1.	 Empirical Performance Comparison: The first systematic comparison of an LLM 
comparing its performance with an LLM-enhanced symbolic logic approach for 
statutory inconsistency detection is provided, revealing a performance gap based 
on output accuracy.
2.	 Hybrid Workflow Development: A practical pipeline is established that optimizes 
each technology by positioning LLMs as formalization assistants rather than as 
primary reasoning engines, leveraging each technology’s strengths.
3.	 Methodological Framework: A replicable experimental framework using the 
SWISH environment is developed. This enables transparent, auditable legal rea­
soning with complete reproducibility.
4.	 Performance Evaluation: A novel methodology is introduced to measure LLM 
reasoning performance in uncovering inconsistencies in tax statutes.
In addition, we highlight the important application of this methodology in integrating 
into the legislative drafting process itself. That is, we lay the groundwork for a system 
that can provide feedback to legislative counsel, flagging potential inconsistencies 
during the draft writing process, which could prevent these issues from becoming 
law.

4.2  Research questions
This investigation centers on several distinct yet complementary research questions.
Research Question One (RQ1): Can GPT-4o, when prompted with natural lan­
guage and Prolog-augmented prompts, reliably detect a known inconsistency in IRC

5  Methodology
5.1  Overview of methodology and statutory context
We develop a methodology for detecting a known inconsistency in IRC § 121 by 
combining large language models (LLMs) with deterministic symbolic logic. To 
accomplish this, a two-phase experimental design is formalized. Phase One investi­
gates whether GPT-4o, when prompted directly in natural language and with Prolog-
augmented prompts, can reliably detect the known inconsistency, thereby testing the 
limits of probabilistic reasoning alone. Phase Two evaluates whether LLMs can gen­
erate and refine Prolog code that generalizes across a diverse set of use cases. Further, 
we investigate whether this independently executed implementation in SWISH can 
detect statutory inconsistencies, thereby assessing Prolog’s viability as a formaliza­
tion framework. This design enables a structured comparison between probabilistic 
LLM reasoning and deterministic symbolic approaches.
5.2  Experimental design (RQ1)
This section addresses Research Question 1 (RQ1): whether GPT-4o, using natural 
language prompting and Prolog-augmented prompts, can detect the known inconsis­
tency in IRC § 121.
We first consider Chain-of-Thought (CoT) prompting with and without Prolog 
reasoning. We adhere to both Wei et al. (2022) and Blair-Stanek et al. (2023) guid­
ance on applying a structured methodological approach that guides LLMs explicitly 
through intermediate reasoning steps that mirror human legal analysis. This tech­
nique is particularly relevant for statutory interpretation tasks, where the reasoning 
process consists of identifying applicable provisions, analyzing their interaction, and 
drawing logical conclusions, which is as important as the final determination. Fig­
ure 1 provides a graphical overview of the experimental design for RQ1.
This study focuses on structured chain-of-thought prompting grounded in a single 
legally validated inconsistency in IRC § 121 identified by Lawsky (2026). Because 
the analysis centers on this specific statutory scenario rather than a dataset of incon­
sistencies, fictional few-shot examples were not introduced.

5.2.2  Evaluation rubric (RQ1)
To evaluate the responses generated in RQ1, a two-dimensional scoring system was 
developed for assessing LLM outputs. Building on the LEGALBENCH framework 
(Guha et al. 2023), this scoring system of LLM-generated outputs was designed. First, 
we present the accuracy score, which quantifies overall performance. Second, represent­
ing a central methodological contribution of this study, is the development of the rule 
coverage score. This is significant because in legal analysis, a correct definitive answer 
(e.g., identifying an inconsistency) may be insufficient if the underlying legal justifica­
tion is incomplete or flawed (Guha et al. 2023). The rule coverage score thus measures 
this competency by evaluating whether the AI model identifies and applies all relevant 
statutory provisions necessary to reason over the inconsistency. This moves beyond sim­
ple binary accuracy and provides a granular, qualitative assessment of the model’s rea­
soning process, mirroring the standards of human legal analysis. It distinguishes between 
what may be a correct guess and a well-reasoned conclusion, ensuring that the evaluation 
captures not just what the model concludes, but how it reaches that conclusion.
1. Accuracy Score (0/1)
This is a measure of the correct classifications of tax code interpretation and appli­
cation, encompassing:
	
●
Issue-spotting: Did the system identify the correct tax problem?
	
●
Rule application: Were the tax code sections logically applied to the facts?
	
●
Rule conclusion: Was the outcome valid and well-supported?
	
●
Interpretation quality: Were ambiguous terms handled appropriately?

5.3  Experimental design (RQ2)
To address Research Question 2, a two-phase methodology was developed to evalu­
ate the viability of LLM-assisted formalization of IRC § 121 for statutory inconsis­
tency detection.
5.3.1  Phase 1
In the first phase, using GPT-4o, the Prolog code developed in addressing Research 
Question 1 (Prompting with Prolog; Appendix 1) was further refined in the SWISH 
environment. The objective of this phase was to ensure that the Prolog code could 
reproduce and detect inconsistencies across a variety of fact patterns generated with 
the assistance of GPT-4o. These additional scenarios encompassed different combi­
nations of ownership duration, use periods, and prior-exclusion intervals under § 121 
and were added as facts to the Prolog code (Appendix 2).
5.3.2  Phase 2
In the second phase, the enhanced Prolog code generated from Phase 1 (Appendix 
2) was further enhanced, this time with GPT-5, which was utilized as a computa­
tional collaborator, identifying potential structural and logical discrepancies between 
the Prolog code and the tax-inconsistency modeling presented in the GitHub reposi­
tory (Lawsky, n.d.). That modeling, implemented in Catala and Python with the Z3 
theorem approver, served as a comparative benchmark for aligning the Prolog model 
with formally verified representations of § 121. This comparative analysis surfaced 
four major deficiencies in the earlier implementation, each of which was corrected

5.3.3  Validation
After the Prolog model began producing more accurate and replicable results, a set 
of validation runners (small, automated test programs) was developed to evaluate 
performance. The validation framework comprised multiple runners designed col­
lectively to test four key properties of the model: whether outcomes were correct, 
internally consistent, deterministic, and capable of autonomously detecting incon­
sistencies. These evaluation metrics, adapted from prior research in computational 
legal reasoning and hybrid neuro-symbolic systems (Bench-Capon and Sartor 2003; 
Garcez et al. 2019), provided a systematic way to assess whether LLM-assisted 
formalization could generate legally faithful, logically coherent, and reproducible 
results.
Finally, to verify the Prolog model’s accuracy, a replication step was conducted 
using Lawsky’s Z3 theorem-prover implementation. This framework systematically 
tests combinations of the statute’s undefined parameters, including the temporal 
unit of the two-year test and the method for calculating the joint numerator, which 
represents the combined qualifying period used to compute a couple’s joint exclu­
sion under § 121(c)(2)(A). Because the statute does not specify how to merge each 
spouse’s ownership, use, and prior-exclusion periods, the implementation evaluates 
alternative rules based on the minimum, maximum, or average of the two spouses’ 
periods. The Prolog model was extended with the same parameterization to replicate 
these logic-level comparisons and verify whether both systems produce identical pat­
terns of consistency and divergence.
The complete Prolog code, including all validation runners, is provided in Appen­
dix 3. The results of all validation experiments are presented in the Findings section.

6.2.1  Structural deficiencies identified in the baseline Prolog formalization
As described in the research methodology above, GPT-5 was prompted to identify

1. Missing dual interpretation structure (§ 121(b)(2)(A) vs. § 121(b)(2)(B))
The Prolog code encoded only one interpretation, the “sum of prorated individ­
ual limits, “and could not therefore evaluate the divergence between § 121(b)(2)(A) 
(joint cap) and § 121(b)(2)(B) (sum of limitations). Without both interpretations rep­
resented, the model could not detect when the two provisions produced inconsistent 
results for the same fact pattern. The revised Prolog version introduced a dual inter­
pretation framework that modeled both readings in parallel, enabling the solver to 
compare their outputs and flag divergence whenever outcomes differed.
2. Omitted “shortest of three” rule (§ 121(c)(1)(B))
Under § 121(c)(1)(B), the allowable exclusion must be prorated according to the 
shortest of three periods: ownership, use, and time since the last exclusion. The base­
line code failed to enforce this requirement, applying proration only to the use period. 
As a result, it overstated eligibility for taxpayers with shorter ownership or prior 
exclusion intervals. The revised Prolog code corrected this by computing the mini­
mum of all three periods (the “shortest of three” rule) before applying the 24-month 
test, ensuring the exclusion ratio aligned precisely with § 121(c)’s temporal logic.
3. Misapplied trigger for proration (§ 121(c))
The baseline implementation treated the presence of any qualifying reason (such 
as employment change, health, or unforeseen circumstances) as an automatic trigger 
for proration, even when the taxpayer already satisfied the full two-out-of-five-year 
test. This produced incorrect reductions in exclusion amounts, contradicting § 121(c)
(1)(A), which limits proration to cases where the time requirement has not been met. 
The revised code explicitly conditioned proration on failure to satisfy the two-year 
test, ensuring that the reduced exclusion applied only under § 121(c) rather than § 
121(a).
4. Collapsed joint return conditions (§ 121(b)(2)(A))
The baseline version allowed one spouse’s ownership and use history to satisfy 
both spouses’ eligibility under § 121(b)(2)(A), collapsing distinct statutory con­
ditions (ownership, use, and prior exclusion limits) that apply separately to each 
taxpayer filing jointly. This masked ambiguity in how the joint cap interacts with 
individual time tests obscures potential conflicts between § 121(b)(2)(A)’s aggregate 
$500,000 limit and § 121(c)(2)(A)’s proportional reduction. The revised implemen­
tation introduced explicit joint numerator modes (such as min of six, min three joint, 
and held b2A months), allowing systematic comparison of alternative joint return 
interpretations.

6.2.2  Validation results
After the errors described above were corrected, GPT-5 was utilized to design and 
refine a series of validation runners (as outlined in the Methodology section). In 
logic-based systems such as Prolog, a runner is an executable unit that loads specific 
facts and initiates the reasoning process under controlled conditions.
Two types of runners were implemented to establish the model’s internal valid­
ity: fixed and mixed. The fixed runners confirmed that the Prolog model accurately 
reproduced established benchmark outcomes under controlled statutory scenarios, 
validating baseline correctness and internal consistency. The mixed runner extended 
this validation framework by integrating fixed and automated procedures within a 
single execution sequence.
Beginning with correctness and determinism checks, the mixed runner then 
invoked CLP(FD)-based search predicates (Codognet & Díaz, 1996) to generate 
additional fact patterns that satisfy statutory constraints. In these predicates, owner­
ship periods, use periods, and prior-exclusion intervals were modeled as constrained 
variables within bounded domains. Using Prolog’s labeling procedure, the system 
systematically enumerated combinations of these variables and instantiated them as 
case facts, which were then evaluated by the Prolog rule set. This process enabled 
automated discovery of divergence scenarios beyond the manually constructed test 
cases, allowing the model to explore previously untested regions of the statutory 
parameter space. Together, these validation runners confirmed that the model satisfies 
four key properties of a sound formal reasoning system: correctness, internal consis­
tency, determinism, and automated inconsistency detection (Bench-Capon and Sartor 
2003; Garcez et al. 2019).

Automated Range Validation (run_joint_prior_table):
This phase extended the automated testing process by systematically varying one 
spouse’s prior-exclusion period from 1 to 36 months while keeping all other eligi­
bility conditions constant. For each configuration, Prolog computed four exclusion 
totals: the aggregate of individual limits (SumA) and three joint-cap interpretations 
(min_six, min_three_joint, and held_b2A_months).
SumA represents the total exclusion under the sum of limitations interpretation 
(§ 121(b)(2)(B)), calculated as the sum of each spouse’s prorated or full $250,000 
allowance. min_six applies the strictest “minimum-of-six-tests” rule, which evalu­
ates the smallest ownership, use, and prior-exclusion periods across both spouses and 
their corresponding time-test combinations to determine eligibility. min_three_joint 
uses a hybrid numerator combining the maximum ownership period with the mini­
mum use and prior-exclusion periods, while held_b2A_months implements an own­
ership-gated mode that first checks for joint ownership and then applies the shorter of 
the use or prior-exclusion months.
The results revealed a distinct shift: outcomes differed below 24 months, aligned 
at 24 months, and stayed identical thereafter (Table 3). This revealed an inconsis­
tency zone concentrated in shorter prior-exclusion intervals, demonstrating that the 
Prolog model can trace the statute’s boundary conditions and reproduce the transition 
from inconsistency to stability across time.
Table 3 summarizes how exclusion amounts change as the prior-exclusion period 
(P) varies from 1 to 36 months. The results reveal three distinct behavioral ranges.
For P = 1–23 months, the SumA column consistently exceeds all three joint-cap 
interpretations (min_six, min_three_joint, and held_b2A_months). This means that 
when one spouse’s prior exclusion occurred less than two years earlier, the couple’s 
combined individual entitlements surpass the $500,000 joint cap. This divergence 
creates an inconsistency zone, where § 121(b)(2)(A) and § 121(c)(2)(A) yield differ­
ent outcomes.
At P = 12 reproduces the ground-truth inconsistency case identified in the fixed-
runner tests, serving as the midpoint example within this zone and confirming that the 
automatically generated results align with manually validated scenarios.
At P = 24 months, all columns converge at $500,000, indicating that both spouses 
now meet the two-year requirement and the statutory interpretations produce identi­
cal results. This alignment marks the boundary where the statute transitions from 
inconsistency to consistency.
For P values beyond 24 months (up to 36 months), all values remain fixed at 
$500,000 across columns, demonstrating that once the two-year condition is met, the 
law behaves consistently, and no further divergence occurs. This confirmed that the 
Prolog system’s reasoning aligned not only with human-coded benchmarks but also 
with independently rediscovered statutory boundaries.

identified inconsistency patterns consistent with theoretical expectations. Collec­

This study represents a conceptual advance in hybrid neuro-symbolic reasoning

7  Discussion and future work
Our findings establish a clear hierarchy for tax law consistency detection. A determin­
istic symbolic reasoner should anchor the analysis, with LLMs providing valuable 
assistance in prompt augmentation and rule formalization. This represents a signifi­
cant departure from approaches that position LLMs as primary reasoning engines for 
legal analysis. Instead, LLMs function most effectively as assistive translators and 
augmenters, enabling rapid conversion of legal text into structured logic that sym­
bolic systems can execute, audit, and verify.
7.1  Empirical evidence for symbolic superiority
The performance gap between approaches is stark and consistent. Symbolic reason­
ing using Prolog deterministically produced the correct outcome in all tested scenar­
ios, achieving a 100% success rate while providing fully auditable reasoning logic. 
In contrast, probabilistic reasoning using GPT-4o was highly prompt-sensitive. Only 
one of the three prompts (33% success rate) elicited the correct identification of the 
inconsistency, and the quality of reasoning varied considerably across prompts.
These results are consistent with prior empirical studies showing that large lan­
guage models perform well in tasks involving legal text interpretation but are less 
reliable when reasoning over interacting statutory rules. Previous evaluations of 
LLMs in legal reasoning benchmarks have reported similar limitations in complex 
rule-application tasks (Nay et al. 2024; Fei et al. 2024; Guha et al. 2023). In con­
trast, symbolic rule representations provide deterministic and auditable reasoning 
processes, making them particularly well suited for evaluating the logical interaction 
of statutory provisions.
7.2  Role and limitations of LLMs in the hybrid workflow
Although LLMs did not perform reliably as a primary reasoning engine, their contri­
butions to the formalization of legal rules were substantial. LLMs were particularly 
useful for prompt enhancement and for rapidly translating statutory language into 
initial formal logic structures. The generated Prolog code could be executed in the 
SWISH environment, although it often contained errors and depended strongly on 
prompt formulation. Nevertheless, this code provided a useful foundation for itera­

tive refinement. LLMs also helped identify relevant statutory sections and their rela­
tionships, generate potential test scenarios and edge cases, and assist with debugging 
symbolic rules during the refinement process.
These findings align with prior research on hybrid legal reasoning systems, which 
suggests that LLMs are more effective as tools for extracting or formalizing legal 
knowledge than as fully autonomous reasoning engines (Holzenberger et al. 2020; 
Holzenberger and Durme 2023; Kant et al. 2024, 2025). Our results similarly indicate 
that LLMs provide valuable assistance in generating structured legal rule representa­
tions, while deterministic symbolic systems remain necessary for reliable rule execu­
tion and verification.
At the same time, the experiments revealed several limitations of LLM-based rea­
soning. Performance was inconsistent across similar prompts, and the models strug­
gled to systematically apply complex rule interactions. In addition, LLM outputs did 
not provide genuine reasoning processes and lacked deterministic audit trails for the 
reasoning steps they produced. These limitations reinforce the importance of combin­
ing probabilistic language models with deterministic symbolic reasoning systems in 
legal analysis.
7.3  Methodological contribution
This study demonstrates how logic-programming–based inconsistency tables can 
systematically expose incompatible legal outcomes arising from the interaction 
of statutory provisions, addressing a recognized limitation of legislative drafting, 
namely, the difficulty of anticipating all normative consequences within complex 
legal systems (Araszkiewicz and Płeszka 2015). Rather than attempting to predict 
legislative outcomes or resolve statutory ambiguity, the proposed approach provides 
a diagnostic method for identifying incompatible outcomes that arise under identical 
factual conditions. The inconsistency tables generated through the Prolog implemen­
tation reveal narrow zones in which IRC § 121 produces divergent exclusion amounts 
across structured variations of statutory conditions. Such zones of instability would 
be difficult to identify through manual drafting review.
7.4  Implications for legal technology development
These findings suggest a clear pipeline for legal AI systems. In the first stage, LLM-
assisted formalization can serve as a viable starting point for translating natural lan­
guage statutes into formal representations. LLMs can assist in rapid prototyping and 
rule generation while also supporting debugging and refinement of symbolic rules 
and program logic. In the second stage, symbolic execution and verification should 
take place in deterministic environments. Formal rules can be executed in symbolic 
reasoning systems, enabling the generation of complete audit trails for legal reason­
ing and ensuring reproducible and explainable results.

7.5  Practical applications
This hybrid approach has several direct practical applications. In legislative draft­
ing, automated consistency checking could identify conflicts in statutory language 
before enactment. In tax compliance, symbolic reasoning systems could systemati­
cally evaluate interactions among complex regulatory provisions. In legal education, 
formal rule representations could provide transparent demonstrations of statutory 
reasoning processes. Finally, in regulatory analysis, the approach could support com­
prehensive consistency checks across complex legal frameworks.
7.6  Limitations and future research
This research has several important limitations that suggest directions for future 
investigation.
First, limitations arise from the prompt engineering and evaluation design used 
for RQ1. Prompts were iteratively refined with guidance from a legal expert, which 
improved specificity but may also have introduced bias into the prompt design. In 
addition, the evaluation scale involved a limited number of test scenarios and relied 
on a single human evaluator with legal training. Future work could expand the eval­
uation framework by incorporating larger sets of statutory scenarios and multiple 
evaluators, thereby strengthening the robustness of the assessment of LLM reasoning 
performance in statutory analysis.
Second, testing was focused on a single section of the Internal Revenue Code 
and one type of inconsistency. While this allowed for a focused analysis based on a 
legally validated scenario, future research could expand the scope of the study by test­
ing whether the hybrid approach can formalize and analyze entire subchapters of the 
IRC or other complex regulatory frameworks, thereby validating the scalability and 
generalizability of the proposed pipeline. Additional studies may also apply the same 
methodology to other sections of the Internal Revenue Code to evaluate whether the 
neuro-symbolic approach is effective across a broader range of legal inconsistencies, 
including the types of conflicts identified by Donelson (2019), such as reconcilable 
inconsistencies or conflicts between justification and execution. A human-in-the-loop 
approach will remain essential for guiding LLMs by using ground-truth cases to 
highlight inconsistency issues and support the formalization of legal rules.
Beyond verifying known inconsistencies, future work could explore discovering 
previously unknown conflicts by developing systematic Prolog queries capable of 
identifying logical contradictions such as conflicting obligations, circular references, 
or impossible conditions across large corpora of formalized rules. Such capabilities 
could transform the methodology from a verification tool into a discovery engine.
Another extension involves integrating the proposed methodology into legislative 
drafting processes. A system capable of providing real-time feedback to legislative 
counsel by flagging potential inconsistencies during drafting could help prevent such 
issues from becoming law. Research into user-friendly interaction in natural language 
and interface design would be essential to support Prolog-based formalization and 
integration between LLMs and symbolic reasoning engines.

Future work may also complement the Prolog pipeline with knowledge graph 
frameworks to enable LLMs to reason over statutory hierarchical relationships. 
Because legal documents such as the Internal Revenue Code are inherently hierarchi­
cal, representing statutory provisions as nodes and relationships such as “is a subsec­
tion of,” “modifies,” or “references” as edges in a knowledge graph could support 
more structured reasoning over interacting legal provisions.
Finally, several limitations relate to the use of LLMs in this study. Because testing 
was conducted through the ChatGPT interface, system-level configurations such as 
temperature, token limits, and internal prompt structures could not be controlled. In 
addition, experiments for RQ2 were conducted sequentially using two different mod­
els, reflecting the rapid development of LLM technologies during the research period. 
The refinement stage therefore represents a methodological evolution rather than a 
strict replication, and results might differ if the entire experiment was repeated using 
a single model. Future research should conduct systematic comparisons across dif­
ferent LLM models and architectures to determine which are best suited for legal for­
malization and prompt augmentation tasks within hybrid symbolic–LLM workflows.
8  Conclusions
The potential benefits of deploying large language models (LLMs) in tax law appli­
cations are substantial. Improving compliance, the avoidance of potentially unfair 
outcomes, and aid in drafting consistent legislation are important benefits. However, 
empirical research on tax-specific LLM applications remains sparse. This research 
demonstrates that while LLMs provide valuable assistance in translating statutory 
language into formal rules, deterministic symbolic reasoning systems are essential 
for reliable statutory inconsistency detection. This finding suggests a complementary 
division between probabilistic LLMs (for formalization and hypothesis generation) 
and deterministic symbolic reasoners (for verification and execution).
Detecting statutory inconsistencies is a legal reasoning problem that involves both 
natural-language processing and formal logic. The finding that GPT-4o succeeded in 
only one of three prompting strategies (a 33% prompt success rate) suggests that it 
struggles to reliably detect inconsistencies on its own. Its reasoning becomes mark­
edly more reliable when augmented with structured chain-of-thought prompting. 
These findings suggest that CoT prompting improves GPT-4o’s response accuracy 
when directed toward a known inconsistency in the tax law, but the model struggles 
to detect such an inconsistency independent of human-in-the-loop expert-provided 
guidance. Prompts that included the phrase “whichever is applicable,” led to correct 
responses in both baseline and Prolog-augmented scenarios. However, Prolog rule 
augmentation did not significantly enhance GPT-4o’s reasoning performance when 
embedded within prompts, even though the Prolog rules affected the reasoning pro­
cess of GPT-4o. In contrast, Prolog rules tested independently in the SWISH environ­
ment demonstrated a greater capacity to detect logical inconsistencies, confirming 
the importance of symbolic reasoning tools in legal analysis.
This research also demonstrates the potential of using LLMs to formalize statu­
tory language into Prolog rules for computational analysis. This opens a path for

Appendix A: Representative Prolog encoding of IRC § 121
In the present study, Prolog was used as an intermediate representation for formal­
izing IRC § 121 because it allows statutory rules to be encoded as executable predi­
cates while preserving interpretability. The model represents taxpayer eligibility, pro­
portional reduction, joint-return calculations, and inconsistency detection as distinct 
rules. This structure makes it possible to test the statute across multiple fact patterns 
and to compare competing interpretations under identical factual conditions.
The following excerpts illustrate the structure of the Prolog representation used in 
the study. They are simplified examples intended to demonstrate how statutory rules 
are encoded.
Taxpayer-level exclusion rule 
The first predicate computes the taxpayer-level exclusion amount by applying the 
statutory “shortest-of-three” rule. It aggregates ownership and use periods, retrieves 
the time since any prior exclusion, selects the shortest of the three periods, and pro­
rates the $250,000 exclusion accordingly.
