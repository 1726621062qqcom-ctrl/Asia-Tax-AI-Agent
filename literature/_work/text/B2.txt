Tax treaties and foreign direct investment: a network
approach
Sunghoon Hong1
Published online: 9 April 2018
© Springer Science+Business Media, LLC, part of Springer Nature 2018
Abstract Multinational investors often reduce tax on dividends by using indirect
investment routes. This paper constructs a tax rate matrix to represent a real-world
network of tax treaties between 70 countries and develops a computation algorithm to
study the structure of tax-minimizing (direct or indirect) investment routes in the tax
treaty network. The treaty shopping rate, deﬁned as the difference between the foreign
tax rates of the direct route and a tax-minimizing route, is about 3.66 percentage points
on average. This paper also examines the relationship between FDI and the structure of
tax-minimizing routes. Empirical results show that the existence of a tax-minimizing
direct route is positively and signiﬁcantly related to FDI. The inward FDI stock via a
tax-minimizing direct route is about 2.14 times larger than the inward FDI stock via
a direct route that is not tax-minimizing. By making a direct route tax-minimizing,
countries can encourage FDI via the direct route and reduce treaty shopping.
Keywords Tax treaty network · Tax-minimizing route · Treaty shopping rate ·
Foreign direct investment
JEL Classiﬁcation F23 · H25 · H87

tionship. For instance, Blonigen and Davies (2004) use an indicator variable for the

Researchers take two different approaches to resolve the puzzling relationship
between tax treaties and FDI. The ﬁrst approach is to use ﬁrm-level data to over-

The second approach is to use network optimization techniques to understand
the network effect of tax treaties on FDI. By using the Floyd–Warshall algorithm
to ﬁnd shortest paths (i.e., tax-minimizing routes) between countries in a tax treaty
network, van’t Riet and Lejour (2017) compute tax rates reduced by treaty shopping
and centrality measures for conduit countries. These computed variables turn out to
be signiﬁcantly related to FDI.
In line with the “network” approach, I examine the structure of tax-minimizing
investment routes in a network of tax treaties between 70 countries. This tax treaty
network is represented as a tax rate matrix where each entry is deﬁned as the minimum
withholding tax (WHT) rate on dividends and determined by relevant tax treaties
and tax laws. In this tax treaty network, investors can minimize tax on dividends by
choosing direct investment routes to 26 countries. These countries impose no WHT
on dividends or have an imputation system under which no WHT may be imposed on
dividends. However, investors may be able to minimize tax on dividends by choosing
indirect investment routes to the rest of the 44 countries.

to their home countries through tax-minimizing indirect routes. OECD (2015) high-

the Base Erosion and Proﬁt Shifting (BEPS) project.

or indirect) investment routes to the 44 countries. My algorithm works under the

owned and operated Chinese subsidiaries through foreign equity-holding companies

From a broader perspective, this paper contributes to the literature on taxation and
investment of multinational ﬁrms. A cental issue in this literature has been whether
and how taxation affects FDI.5 More recently, researchers focus on how tax systems,
together with nontax motives, inﬂuence location decisions and ownership structures of
multinational ﬁrms. Barrios et al. (2012) ﬁnd that corporate taxation of foreign-source
dividends in residence countries may deter entries into potential source countries.6
Mintz and Weichenrieder (2010) ﬁnd that German multinational ﬁrms use indirect
ownership structures through conduit countries and suggest that treaty shopping is
a main reason for indirect structures. Lewellen and Robinson (2013) also discover
that American multinational ﬁrms use indirect ownership structures for tax motives.
Dyreng et al. (2015) ﬁnd that American multinational ﬁrms are more likely to use
indirect ownership structures (i.e., foreign equity-holding companies) as the WHT
rate on dividends paid from a source country to the United States increases. However,
they ﬁnd no evidence that the existence of a tax treaty reduces the use of indirect
structures.

2 Network approach
A complete directed network is a network in which each pair of nodes is connected by
two links with opposite directions. Countries i and j can be thought of as nodes i and
j in a complete directed network where each link ij is given a weight tij representing
a withholding tax (WHT) rate on dividends. This complete directed network can be
thought of as a tax treaty network because tax treaties often determine WHT rates on
dividends paid across countries.7 By constructing a matrix T = [tij] to represent a tax
treaty network, and by developing a computation algorithm, I examine the structure
of tax-minimizing investment routes in the tax treaty network.
2.1 Investment routes
An investor plans to invest in country s but lives in country h. From this investment,
the investor will earn income m as dividends in country s and repatriate her income
to country h. Country s is called the source country, while country h is called the
residence (or home) country.
The investor chooses an investment route to maximize her net-of-tax income in
the residence country, i.e., to minimize tax when she remits her income to country h.
Formally, an investment route, or simply a route, is deﬁned as a series of countries,
h, i, . . . , j, s, from country h to country s. Let R(h, s) denote the set of all routes from
h to s. Let r ∈R(h, s) denote a route. Given a route r = h, i, . . . , j, s, countries i
through j are called pass-through countries. A route h, i, . . . , j, s is often written as
h →i →· · · →j →s
when it is necessary to highlight the direction of the route. The investor can choose
an indirect route h, i, . . . , j, s to invest in country s by establishing conduit entities in
countries i through j and making her investment indirectly through these entities. She
can also choose the direct route h, s to invest in country s. When the investor remits her
income from s to h, the remittance route follows the reverse order of the countries in the
investment route. The investor leaves no retained earnings in pass-through countries.
The investor’s net-of-tax income depends on tax relief rules in the home country. I
assume that the home country has a deduction or an exemption system. The foreign
tax rate f (r) of a route r is deﬁned as follows:

has such an incentive if the residence country has a deduction or an exemption system.
2.2 Tax rate matrix
A tax rate matrix T = [tij] shows WHT rates on dividends paid across national
borders.9 Each WHT rate tij is applied for a pair of countries i and j. If an entity
in country i remits dividends to an entity in country j, the tax agency in country i
imposes WHT on dividends at the rate tij. Each WHT rate tij is determined by the tax
treaty between countries i and j and by the national tax law of country i. For the sake
of running an algorithm, each diagonal entry tii is assumed to be zero.
I construct a tax rate matrix T = [tij] between 70 countries, as listed in Tables 1 and
2, by using the information on tax treaties and tax laws from Deloitte International
Tax Source and PwC Worldwide Tax Summaries.10 From these Web sites, I also ﬁnd
the information on CIT rates. Tables 1 and 2 summarize notable WHT systems with
minimum, median, and maximum WHT rates and CIT rates in the 70 countries, as of
July 2015.
Each WHT rate tij is determined as follows:
First, if the European Union Parent-Subsidiary Directive (EU PSD) can be applied
for countries i and j, then set tij = 0. The EU PSD requires the exemption of WHT
on dividends paid across EU countries, Iceland, Norway, and Switzerland.11
Second, if the EU PSD cannot be applied for at least one of countries i and j, then
check if there is a tax treaty between countries i and j. If there is no tax treaty between
i and j, then set tij equal to the WHT rate speciﬁed by the national tax law of country
i.

Subnational (state, province, etc.) governments may also impose WHT on divi-

idends, the rate is usually very low. Therefore, I only consider WHT imposed by
national (federal) governments.

WHT may be imposed on dividends distributed by companies that already pay CIT.

countries impose no WHT on dividends.

is no greater than 15% if dividends are paid to shareholders resident in a country with
a tax treaty.

2.3 Algorithm
Given a tax rate matrix T = [tij] and a pair (h, s) of residence and source countries, the
investor can ﬁnd tax-minimizing routes from country h to country s by using a com-
putation algorithm. I propose a computation algorithm that works under a constraint
on the number of pass-through countries. This algorithm repeats the following three
steps for all pairs of residence and source countries. Let (h, s) be a pair of countries.
Step 1. Build a list of all routes from country h to country s under the constraint that
the number of pass-through countries in each route is at most two and compute
the net-of-tax income for each route.
Step 2. Find the maximum net-of-tax income among all routes in the list.
Step 3. Find and list all the routes from h to s that achieve the maximum net-of-tax
income.
Given 70 countries, there are 4830 (= 70 × 69) pairs of residence and source
countries. Because there are at most two pass-through countries in a route, the list of
all routes includes an indirect route h →i →j →s with two pass-through countries
i and j, an indirect route h →j →s with a pass-through country j, and the direct
route h →s. Given 70 countries, for each pair of residence and source countries, there
are 4625 (= 68 × 67 + 68 + 1) routes in the list of Step 1.
As the maximum number of pass-through countries in a route increases, the number
of routes in the list of Step 1 grows exponentially, and the computational burden (or
running time) of the algorithm increases exponentially. To limit the computational
burden, I assume that there are at most two pass-through countries in a route.12
3 Network results
For an indirect route, the net-of-tax income depends on corporate income tax (CIT)
rates in pass-through countries. I consider two cases about CIT rates. In the ﬁrst case
(R), every pass-through country imposes CIT at a reduced rate of 1%.13 In the second
case (S), every pass-through country imposes CIT at the statutory rate. One can expect
that tax-minimizing indirect routes tend to pass through countries with no WHT in
case (R), and pass through countries with no CIT in case (S).

source countries is deﬁned as the difference between the foreign tax rates of the
direct route and a tax-minimizing route.14 An investor can reduce her foreign tax rate

tax-minimizing, the treaty shopping rate is zero. If not, the treaty shopping rate is a
positive number in percentage points.

by PASSij, is deﬁned as the percentage of tax-minimizing indirect routes to j passing

tax-minimizing indirect route to j in case (R), let PASSij = 0. Similarly, let PASSSij
denote the pass-through rate of i to j in case (S).

to the notion of betweenness centrality in van’t Riet and Lejour (2017). To see which
countries are common pass-through countries in tax-minimizing indirect routes, I will

4 Empirical application
Multinational ﬁrms can choose investment (ownership) structures that inﬂuence for-
eign direct investment (FDI) between countries at an aggregate level. If such investors
consider tax as an important factor when choosing investment structures, the results
of the network analysis in Sect. 3 will be consistent with FDI data. For instance, Mintz

discover that multinational ﬁrms use indirect ownership structures for tax motives.
Here, I investigate whether the network variables in Sect. 3 help explain the patterns
of FDI by using Ordinary Least Squares (OLS) and Poisson Pseudo Maximum Like-
lihood (PPML) estimation methods. Silva and Tenreyro (2006) propose the PPML
estimation to deal with numerous zero values in trade data. Because FDI data also
exhibit a similar pattern, I adopt the PPML estimation to complement the log-linear

4.1 Data and variables
For a pair of countries i and j, FDIij is deﬁned as the inward FDI stock from i to j in
millions of US dollars. I use the bilateral FDI data of the UNCTAD that are compiled
on the basis of immediate ownership relations.23 Because the network variables are
calculated without time-series dimension, I focus on cross-sectional data for year 2012
or the latest year available.24 For instance, I use the FDI data of Luxembourg for year
2011 because the data for 2012 is unavailable. Because all the 70 countries except
for Gibraltar (GI) and Guernsey (GG) are included in the bilateral FDI data, there are
4556 (= 68 × 67) pairs of countries.
Table 10 provides descriptive statistics for regression variables. Note that the min-
imum value of FDIij is negative and there are 86 observations with negative FDIij in
the data.25 Also, there are 2141 observations with zero FDIij. To keep the observations
with zero FDIij, the natural logarithm of inward FDI stock, denoted by ln FDIij, is cal-
culated as ln(FDIij + 1). Since the log-linear OLS and the PPML estimations exclude
the observations with negative FDIij, there remain 4470 observations in regressions.
Three groups of network variables, DIRECT, TSHOP, and PASS, are introduced
and calculated in Sect. 3. These network variables are deﬁned for a pair of countries
i and j in each case of (R) and (S).
Firstly, each DIRECT variable is an indicator variable for the existence of a tax-
minimizing direct route. In case (R), if there is a tax-minimizing direct route from i
to j, DIRECTij = 1, and otherwise, DIRECTij = 0. Also, DIRECTSij indicates the
existence of a tax-minimizing direct route from i to j in case (S).

I expect that each DIRECT variable is positively related to inward FDI stock. When
the direct route is tax-minimizing between a pair of residence and source countries,
investors will consider the direct route more attractive than indirect routes, and thus,
they will use the direct route more frequently. Hong (2014) develops a game-theoretic
model of treaty shopping and tax auditing between an investor and a tax agency, where
the existence of a tax-minimizing direct route increases the investor’s equilibrium
probability to use the direct route. This equilibrium behavior leads to increased FDI
between residence and source countries at an aggregate level.
Secondly, each TSHOP variable is a continuous variable specifying the difference
between the foreign tax rates of the direct route and a tax-minimizing route. In case (R),
the treaty shopping rate among routes from i to j is deﬁned as TSHOPij = t ji −f (r∗),
where r∗is a tax-minimizing route from i to j. Also, TSHOPSij denotes the treaty
shopping rate among routes from i to j in case (S).
I expect that each TSHOP variable is negatively related to inward FDI stock. When
the direct route is not tax-minimizing between a pair of residence and source countries,
a TSHOP variable takes a positive value. Moreover, as the TSHOP variable increases,
by deﬁnition, the difference becomes larger between the foreign tax rates of the direct
route and a tax-minimizing route. Thus, investors will consider the direct route less
attractive than tax-minimizing indirect routes. This can reduce FDI between residence
and source countries at an aggregate level.
Thirdly, each PASS variable is a continuous variable specifying the relative fre-
quency of being a pass-through country in tax-minimizing indirect routes. In case

I expect that each PASS variable is positively related to inward FDI stock. If there

if a country is more likely to be a pass-through country in tax-minimizing indirect
routes to a source country, the portion of indirect investments via the country can be
larger than those via other countries.

and a colonial relationship, respectively. DISTij is deﬁned as the population-weighted
distance between countries i and j in thousands of kilometers. Head et al. (2010) use
these bilateral variables to examine the patterns of international trade. I obtain the data
for these variables from the CEPII.26 In addition, RTAij is an indicator variable for
the existence of a regional trade agreement (RTA) between countries i and j. Egger
and Larch (2008) examine the determinants of a regional trade agreement. I obtain the
RTA data from Mario Larch’s database.27

j, respectively. Because I focus on a cross-sectional analysis, I use GDP data for year

4.2 Results
Now, I present regression results with network variables. Table 11 provides the results
of the OLS regressions with network variables DIRECTij, TSHOPij, and PASSij in
case (R). In the OLS regressions, the dependent variable is ln FDIij, the natural loga-
rithm of inward FDI stock. Each regression includes one of the three network variables
as a main independent variable.31 Each regression also includes bilateral variables
CONTIGij, COMLANGij, COMLEGij, COLONYij, DISTij, and RTAij to control for
the relationship between countries i and j. In addition, to consider the effects of foreign
tax credit systems, columns (1)–(3) include a home-speciﬁc indicator variable FTCi.
Columns (4)–(6) include a pair-speciﬁc indicator variable SFTCij. Furthermore, to
control for country-speciﬁc factors, columns (1)–(3) include CIT and log-scaled GDP
in home country i and source country j. Columns (4)–(6) include dummy variables
for home and source countries.
Table 11 shows that each of the three network variables DIRECTij, TSHOPij, and
PASSij is signiﬁcantly related to inward FDI stock.

at the 1% level. Column (4) suggests that the inward FDI stock via a tax-minimizing
direct route is 2.14 (= exp(0.76)) times larger than the inward FDI stock via a direct
route that is not tax-minimizing.

at the 1% level. Column (5) suggests that the inward FDI stock decreases by 3.92%
(= exp(−0.04) −1) as the treaty shopping rate increases by one percentage point.
In columns (3) and (6), the coefﬁcients on PASSij are positive and signiﬁcant at
the 1% level. Column (6) suggests that the inward FDI stock increases by 3.05%
(= exp(0.03) −1) as the pass-through rate increases by one percentage point.

route. For instance, to reduce the treaty shopping rate, countries can unilaterally reduce
withholding tax rates on dividends paid to foreign investors.

5 Conclusion
In this paper, I construct a tax rate matrix to represent a tax treaty network and
develop a computation algorithm to examine the structure of tax-minimizing routes.
I ﬁnd that the use of a tax-minimizing indirect route can substantially reduce tax
on dividends incurred by a multinational investor. I also ﬁnd that the existence of a
tax-minimizing direct route is positively related to foreign investment via the direct
route.
When a direct route is not tax-minimizing, to prevent the use of a tax-minimizing
indirect route, i.e., to prevent treaty shopping, countries need to consider negotiating
a new tax treaty to make the direct route tax-minimizing. These countries may not
necessarily experience tax revenue loss, as the new treaty makes the direct route tax-
minimizing and attracts more foreign investment.
For future studies, it will be interesting to study the relationship between tax treaties
and ownership structures of multinational ﬁrms. In this paper, I focused on FDI data
to see the plausibility of the treaty network analysis. However, the aggregate FDI data
do not reveal the actual ownership structures (i.e., investment routes) of multinational
ﬁrms. Hence, to check the plausibility of the network analysis, it is important to
examine whether and how multinational ﬁrms organize indirect ownership structures
when direct ownership structures are not tax-minimizing.
It will also be interesting to study the network effect of LOB (limitation on beneﬁts)
provisions in tax treaties on the structure of tax-minimizing investment routes. In this
paper, when constructing the tax rate matrix, I considered a restrictive version of LOB
provisions that only allow certain pension funds to obtain the beneﬁt of the lowest
withholding tax rate. However, in real-world tax treaties, LOB provisions can impose
various residency requirements. Thus, it is important to examine how LOB provisions
with various residency requirements affect the structure of tax-minimizing investment
routes in tax treaty networks.
Lastly, it will be interesting to study indirect structures that reduce tax on interest
and royalties. In this paper, I concentrated on withholding tax rates on dividends to
describe a tax treaty network. However, tax treaties also affect withholding tax rates on
interest and royalties. Moreover, FDI can take the form of debt as well as equity. FDI
with debt instruments may be inﬂuenced by tax rates on interest as FDI with equity
instruments may be inﬂuenced by tax rates on dividends. Therefore, it is important
to examine how tax treaties affect indirect structures to reduce tax on interest and
royalties and how such indirect structures affect FDI patterns.
