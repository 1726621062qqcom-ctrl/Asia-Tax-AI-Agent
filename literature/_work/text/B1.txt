Abstract The international corporate tax system is considered a network. Just like
for transportation, “shortest” paths are computed, which minimize tax payments for
multinational enterprises when they repatriate proﬁts. We include corporate income
tax rates, withholding taxes on dividends, double tax treaties, and double taxation relief
methods. We ﬁnd that treaty shopping leads to an average potential reduction of the tax
burden on repatriated dividends of about 6% points. An indicator for centrality in the
tax network identiﬁes the UK, Luxembourg, and the Netherlands as the most important
conduit countries. Low-tax havens do not have a crucial role in reducing dividend
repatriation taxes. By contrast, tax haven ﬁnancial centres do. In the regressions we
ﬁnd that the centrality measures are robustly signiﬁcant explanatory variables for
bilateral FDI stocks. This also holds for our treaty shopping indicator.

1 Introduction
Treaty shopping is the practice whereby multinational enterprises (MNEs), rather than
investing directly in a host country, redirect the investment through a third country to
take advantage of treaty provisions not found between the host and the home country of
the investment (Davies 2004). These provisions are formulated in bilateral tax treaties
to avoid double taxation of corporate income and to stimulate mutual foreign direct
investment (FDI). These treaties are referred to as DTTs: double tax treaties. MNEs
use these treaties to lower their tax burden.
Little seems to be known quantitatively about treaty shopping as no readily available
measure exists for its magnitude or the subsequent reduction of the MNE’s effective
tax burden. In this paper we examine the extent to which treaty shopping may lead to
FDI diversion and reduced repatriation tax rates on distributed dividends. This requires
the construction of measures, or indicators, of treaty shopping. Circumventing the lack
of direct data, we will construct these from publicly available national tax parameters.
We examine whether these indicators contribute to explaining observed bilateral FDI
positions.

economies account for large shares in FDI. For example, total inward FDI stocks into
the Netherlands in 2013 equalled USD 4263 billion, accounting for 15% of worldwide
inward FDI stocks. In the same year the Netherlands ranked 17th in terms of GDP
(between brackets) with a share of about 1% of worldwide GDP. For Luxembourg the
discrepancy is even more pronounced, as it is for the British Virgin Islands, ranking

According to OECD (2013), the worldwide pattern of FDI is suggestive of interna-
tional tax planning. Treaty shopping is one of the practices that MNEs use to exploit
the differences in the national tax codes of different jurisdictions. Other such practices
are transfer pricing, thin capitalization, and hybrid mismatches.1
To develop the treaty shopping indicators, we consider the international tax system
as a network. We compute the optimal paths which minimize the tax burden of MNEs
when they repatriate proﬁts. The methodology is similar to determining the shortest
path in a transportation network. The network consists of 108 countries, and the tax
burdens are calculated from the statutory rates of corporate income taxes, withholding
taxes on dividends, and double tax relief methods. Typically, bilateral DTTs lower
reciprocally withholding taxes and provide for more generous relief methods (Avi-
Yonah and Panayi 2011).
We use a multilateral dimension of corporate taxation for dividend ﬂows. In this
respect, we follow Barrios et al. (2012) in combining host and home country taxation,
including tax treaties and double tax relief. They ﬁnd that taxation of the home country,
additional to that of the host country, has a signiﬁcant negative impact on the location
of new subsidiaries. Egger et al. (2009) construct effective tax rates between country
pairs, reﬂecting overall host and home country taxation, and ﬁnd that the bilateral
effective tax rate has a negative impact on bilateral FDI stocks. However, they only
construct these rates for direct routes and do not take account of treaty shopping. Their
sample consists of OECD countries; for these countries the marginal and average
effective tax rates (EMTR and EATR) are available. Lacking the data on effective tax
rates for most of the non-OECD countries in our sample, we use statutory rates (see
also Barrios et al. 2012).
We consider a proﬁt repatriation optimization problem for a given subsidiary in a
host country and the parent company in the home country. On tax-minimizing routes,
which may involve FDI diversion, the tax parameters of other countries matter as
well. All information is compiled and stored in a matrix describing the tax burden for
incoming and outgoing dividends between each pair of the 108 countries. This matrix
is entered into the Floyd–Warshall algorithm from graph theory, which efﬁciently
performs the required minimizations. After a few minor adaptations, the algorithm
computes the optimal tax routes. The difference between the tax costs on the direct
route and on the optimal one yields the treaty shopping gain.
Moreover, the network approach enables us to identify countries most likely to per-
form the role of conduits, countries often accused of being accessories to tax avoidance
by MNEs. A central position in the international tax network is a necessary condition
for the role as a conduit. With a concept from graph theory, we compute measures of
network centrality.
We employ these measures in the econometric analysis. We regress bilateral FDI
stocks on tax variables and the indicator from the network approach on treaty shopping
gain.Inaddition,weaddthenetworkcentralitymeasuresforthehostandhomecountry.

The FDI data are from the IMF CDIS database, and the tax parameters are obtained

Direct evidence of treaty shopping and FDI diversion is scarce for two reasons. First,
the FDI data list the investment from a home country into a host country. However,
this does not imply that the investment stays in the host or that the home country is the
actual origin of the investment. Hence, from these data, diverted and undiverted FDI
cannot be disentangled. Second, treaty shopping may be considered as treaty abuse and
be illegal: the granting of (tax) treaty beneﬁts in inappropriate circumstances (OECD
2015).2 With the possibility of treaty shopping being illegal, direct evidence is even
less likely. There are a few papers attempting to quantify treaty shopping. Weyzig
(2013) makes use of micro-data of Dutch Special Purpose Entities (SPEs) from 2007.
SPEs, in general, are entities with no or few employees, little or no physical presence
in the host country and whose core business consists of group ﬁnancing or holding
activities (OECD 2013). By comparing the FDI ﬂows via Dutch SPEs from a home
to a host country to the direct FDI ﬂows between those countries, derived from the
balance of payment statistics, he estimates the share of bilateral FDI that is passing the
Netherlands. This share is 6% points higher with a tax treaty route via the Netherlands
compared to routes without a treaty. This is a large effect because on average 11% of
worldwide FDI stocks has passed the Netherlands. The low withholding tax rates on
dividends also have a signiﬁcant impact on treaty shopping. Mintz and Weichenrieder
(2010) construct the chains of corporate structures across various countries for German
multinationals and relate these structures to the underlying ﬁscal motives. The level
of withholding taxes is found to be important in determining which countries are used
as a platform for investments.
Wereport threemainﬁndings. First, treatyshoppingpotentiallyleads toasigniﬁcant
reduction of the tax burden of MNEs. That reduction is 6% points on average. Second,
the top 3 conduit countries according to our main measure of network centrality are
the UK, Luxembourg, and the Netherlands. Low-tax havens, i.e. those countries with
a low or zero corporate tax rate, are not relevant for dividend repatriation. Tax haven
ﬁnancial centres, however, are important. Third, the coefﬁcients of the treaty shopping
indicator and the centrality measures are robustly found to be signiﬁcant, statistically
and in terms of economic impact on bilateral FDI stocks. A 1%-point tax gain due to
treaty shopping raises the bilateral FDI stock by 1.0%. An increase of one standard
deviation of the centrality measure raises bilateral FDI stocks by 10–20%. These
results conﬁrm the relevance of the treaty shopping measures derived from the network
approach.
We contribute to the literature with a novel and fundamentally multilateral approach
that pairs a rigorous optimization framework with basic tax parameters. It generates
quantitative indicators on treaty shopping in a network of 108 countries, including tax
havens. With the exception of Hong (2017), we are not aware of other work using
this approach and quantiﬁed at this scale.3 Hong (2017) constructs tax-minimizing
routes for a set of 70 economies, allowing a maximum of two conduit countries only.

He focuses on the treaty-speciﬁc withholding taxes on dividends. The current paper
differs by also including home country taxation. This contributes to the larger reduction
in the repatriation tax burden of 6.0% points, instead of the 3.7% points of Hong
(2017). Although home country taxation is not included in his network analysis, Hong
(2017) does include corporate income tax rates and a dummy for foreign tax credit as
determinants for bilateral FDI stocks in the regression analysis. He also introduces a
variable indicating whether a direct route is optimal or not. This variable proves to be
statistically signiﬁcant.
An important limitation of both papers is the focus on dividends only. The network
analysis takes the investment and proﬁt repatriation decisions between a parent com-
pany and a subsidiary in another country as given. We allow for indirect ﬁnancing
structures by which MNEs reduce the repatriation taxation of dividends. Mintz and
Weichenrieder (2010) refer to this as the treaty shopping motive for setting up conduit
entities in third countries. We neglect royalty and interest payments for tax planning
structures, which are instrumental in stripping corporate earnings in high-tax host
countries. The emphasis in this paper is on treaty shopping for dividends, reducing
withholding taxes, and in some cases the taxation in home countries. Still, the network
approach offers a rich multi-country framework to investigate the dividend repatriation
tax rates facing MNEs.4

2 A network approach to international corporate taxation
This section sets out the main methodological framework. The international corporate
tax system is considered as a network of countries where distance is deﬁned as the
tax burden of repatriating corporate income from one country to another country.
First, it is described how these distances are composed from different international
taxes, following Barrios et al. (2012). As our sample contains 108 countries, we have
more than 10 thousand bilateral relations. Next, the tax burdens of these relations
are discussed, involving conduit countries and treaty shopping. Third, it is shown
how the tax burdens ﬁt into efﬁcient algorithms for computing shortest paths over the
whole network.5 Hence, the shortest paths are optimal tax routes. Fourth, a measure
of network centrality is introduced to identify the most used conduit countries.

2.1 Tax burden, treaty shopping, and a network algorithm
Consider a multinational with a subsidiary in a host country S and a parent company in
home country P. Both countries may tax the income of the subsidiary. First, the host
country levies a corporate income tax (CIT), at a rate tS. Next, the host country may
also levy a general non-resident withholding tax on the dividends of the subsidiary.
The tax is denoted with wS. However, the host and home country may have signed a
tax treaty and a preferential rate wSP ≤wS may apply. The home country may tax
the foreign-source income at its CIT rate, tP.
The tax code of the home country may contain provisions to avoid double taxation.
For instance, it may have a dividend participation exemption: under certain conditions
all, or part, of the foreign-source dividend income is exempted from the corporate
income tax. These conditions typically require a minimum share in the subsidiary and
a minimum number of years that the shares are held. Some countries do not apply
tax relief to dividend from low-tax countries or tax havens (CFC rules). Apart from
exemption two other methods of double tax relief are taken into account: deduction
and credits.6 Deduction is the most modest relief method where no taxes need to be
paid over the taxes already paid. The latter are deducted from the tax base in the home
country. With the credit system the tax base is the income of the subsidiary, but the
corporate income tax is credited with taxes paid in the host country.7 Excess credit is
not restituted. The credit method is less generous than exemption, but more so than
deduction.
Let rm P be the general double tax relief method applied by home country P. The
tax treaties the country has signed may contain agreements to provide more generous
double tax relief to treaty partner S. Thus, also the relief methods have a double country
dimension: rmSP. The resulting combined tax rates after applying the relief method
are fully in line with Barrios et al. (2012).

2.2 A centrality measure for conduit countries
The lowest tax burden for a particular country pair may be incurred on the direct
route or on an indirect one. In the latter case the route contains intermediate coun-
tries. We identify these conduit countries using a centrality measure from network
theory: betweenness. Given all optimal paths between all country pairs in the network,
betweenness centrality is deﬁned as follows: for a given country, count the number of
times it is on an optimal path from S to P. Divide this number by the total number
of optimal paths between S and P. Then sum these fractions over all pairs S and P,
excluding those pairs where the given country is the initial host or home.
We have performed the summation over country pairs for the betweenness centrality
measure with double GDP weights. These weights are taken as a proxy for the relative
size of undiverted dividend ﬂows, which ideally would have been used. However,
these are unknown. Although the OECD has some sparse data on reported bilateral
dividend payments, it must be realized that these data are burdened with the central

tax minimization will make sure that the chosen routes include as much as possible
countries with the dividend participation exemption. A third and practical reason for
not using effective tax rates is that these are simply not available for most countries in
our selection.

4 Double taxation and double tax relief
The bilateral direct dividend repatriation tax rates combine the withholding tax on
dividends and taxation in the home country, as explained in Sect. 2. This section
presents descriptive statistics on double tax relief.
A multinational could face double taxation when repatriating proﬁts of a foreign
subsidiary. Taking the CIT of the host country as given, there remains in theory about
41% additional, i.e. double, taxation.20 In practice double tax relief methods and tax
treaties reduce or even eliminate double taxation; the combined effect is that there
remains a world average double taxation of 12%. We conclude that double tax relief
methods and tax treaties do what they are supposed to do: they reduce double taxation
substantially, by almost 30% points.
Double taxation varies by country. We average the bilateral direct dividend repa-
triation tax rates for the countries as home or as host of the investments. Low average
tax rates for inbound dividends make a country attractive for corporate residence. The
Netherlands and Finland head the ranking of countries in this respect, with average
rates of 3.4 and 3.7%; see Table 5. By contrast, the average rate for the USA is 16.7%.
This implies that when US corporations would switch their legal residence to one of
these European countries they could reduce the tax burden on dividends from all over
the world by more than 10% points. Ireland, often a candidate country for such a tax
inversion, has an average repatriation tax rate of 5.6% and ranks 11th.21
Exemption contributes to a low average inbound rate, certainly compared to coun-
tries with only credits for taxes already paid. The number of bilateral tax treaties also
matters for the inbound rates as they stipulate reduced withholding taxes for the host
country of the repatriated proﬁts. The low-tax havens in general have no or few double
tax treaties and therefore do not rank high. This is markedly different for the tax haven
ﬁnancial centres.

withholding tax rate in China do not exceed the CIT of the USA. The repatriation tax
rate therefore is equal to the difference between the CIT rates, expressed on the tax
base after the CIT in China, amounting to 18.8%.23 Below we will discover that this
rate cannot be lowered by treaty shopping.

6.1 Multiplicity of shortest paths and alternative centrality measures
Centrality measures are calculated using all optimal paths. The total number of optimal
paths can be much larger than the number of country pairs. In fact, multiplicity of
optimal paths in the international corporate tax network is abundant. A potential source
of huge multiplicity is direct routes with a zero tax rate; no double taxation at all on

cost detours have been countered by introducing a small penalty, initially epsilon, for
each additional conduit country on a route. This reduces the average multiplicity, but
it is still about 5.5 paths per country pair.

A well-known problem in estimating gravity equations is the number of zero values
for the dependent variable. This problem is extensively discussed in the trade literature
(Head and Mayer 2014) with the many zeros for bilateral trade values in samples
with developing countries. The zero values create missing values with a standard log
transformation. This also applies for bilateral FDI stocks. The most common solution
is a transformation of log(1+x) in which x is the bilateral trade value. This does not
solve all problems. Because trade values cannot be negative, the error distribution is
truncated and could depend on the explanatory variables. Santos-Silva and Terenyro
(2006) argue for that reason to use a Poisson pseudo-maximum likelihood (PPML)
method. The problem of our data sample is the extremely skewed distribution of the
FDI values ranging from many zero values to values of more than 100 billion USD.
About 1% of the observations exceeds 100 billion USD, and these cover 50% of the
value of the aggregated bilateral FDI stocks in the sample.32 To correct for the skewed
distribution, we use the log transformation in an OLS estimation and present the results
of the PPML estimator as a robustness analysis.
Another issue is the potential endogeneity of various explanatory variables with
bilateral FDI stocks. The literature on the impact of bilateral tax treaties on FDI treats
these treaties often as endogenous33; increased investment could raise the necessity of
a treaty. Many conduit countries indeed signed many treaties. Moreover, the withhold-
ing tax rates could be endogenous. Chisik and Davies (2004) show theoretically and
empirically that larger asymmetric FDI between countries implies higher tax rates.
However, since the network centrality of a conduit country is measured as an average
over all its 107 partner countries, the impact of a speciﬁc bilateral treaty and its nego-
tiated tax rates on the network centrality of a country is only limited. This suggests
that the potential endogeneity between network centrality and bilateral FDI is very
indirect and small and therefore of minor importance in this study.

7.2 Robustness analysis
The coefﬁcients of the centrality indicators for the home and host country have a
positive and signiﬁcant impact on bilateral FDI stocks. Moreover, we also ﬁnd that
bilateral FDI stocks are larger if the direct proﬁt repatriation route is also the optimal
tax route. We verify that these conclusions also hold for other speciﬁcations.
First, we estimate the impact of the treaty shopping gain with ﬁxed effects for
the home and host countries. This excludes the possibility of omitted country-speciﬁc
variables, but also throws the baby out with the bathwater. The centrality indicators are
our maininterest. Theyarecountryspeciﬁcandtherefore“included”intheﬁxedeffects
in this speciﬁcation. The coefﬁcient for the treaty shopping gain is still negative and
signiﬁcant at the 90% level. Due to the correlation with the other bilateral tax variables
discussed above, the standard errors are quite large now. As an alternative we have
replaced the treaty shopping gain by a dummy, which is one if the direct route is the
optimal one and zero if it is not. Hong (2017) also uses this dummy. This reduces the
correlation and the coefﬁcient for BWHT becomes signiﬁcant. The coefﬁcient for the
dummy is positive and signiﬁcant.35
Second, we apply the PPML estimator instead of OLS. The dependent variable
is FDI and includes all zero values. Now, the variance of the dependent variable is
much larger, because the log transformation reduces the skewed distribution of the
FDI values ranging from many zero values to values below 100 billion USD. About
1% of the observations exceeds 100 billion USD and covers 50% of the value of the
aggregated bilateral FDI stocks in sample. The coefﬁcients of treaty shopping gain
and the centrality indicators are still signiﬁcant and have the expected sign. Neither
the coefﬁcient for the CIT rate in the host country nor the coefﬁcient for the bilateral
withholding tax is signiﬁcant now. The coefﬁcient of the remaining CIT rate in the
home country is positive. The log transformation reduces the impact of the large FDI
stocks of the US, which are positively correlated with the high effective tax rates in
the US. With PPML, we ﬁnd a large correlation between bilateral FDI and these high
effective tax rates. The effects of the treaty shopping gain on FDI are larger, and the
centrality indicator in the home country also has more impact on FDI (Table 13).

8 Conclusion
We have presented a novel perspective by applying a network analysis to international
corporatetaxation.ThisyieldsthecontributionofFDIdiversion,andthecorresponding
dividend ﬂows, to the reduction of the tax burden of multinational enterprises. At the
same time it provides insight into the central position of particular countries in the
international tax network. We have modelled corporate taxation in host and home
countries, double tax relief methods, and the withholding tax on dividends for all pairs
in a sample of 108 countries. The direct effect of double tax relief and tax treaties
lowers the world average repatriation tax rate from a theoretical 41–12%.
Our ﬁrst result is that the possibility of treaty shopping allows for a further reduction
of 6% points, leaving, on average, a double taxation on repatriated dividends of 6%.
For about two-thirds of the country pairs examined, there exists an indirect tax route
that is more attractive in terms of lower taxes than the direct route. A large cluster of
68 countries exists that are well interconnected through zero tax routes. These results
imply that it can be very beneﬁcial for multinational companies to restructure their
investments over various countries in such a way that tax payments are minimized.
Centrality in the network is used to identify candidates for the role of conduit coun-
try. This leads to the second result. The UK heads the ranking of network centrality,
followed by Luxembourg and the Netherlands. The top 10 has only one non-EU coun-
try: Singapore. Ireland is the third tax haven ﬁnancial centre in the top 10. In contrast,

The third result concerns the empirical relevance of our indicators. We regress

array of robustness checks.
This brings us to the limitations of the study and directions for further research.
We take the proﬁts in the host country as given and focus on dividend ﬂows. More
generally, a broader ﬁscal and juridical environment will affect the holding deci-
sions of multinationals and the size of taxable proﬁt incomes. These activities may
involve intra-company ﬁnancing and the location of intellectual property rights so
that deductibility of interest and royalty payments matter and the withholding taxes
for these categories. We ignore the possibilities to reduce the tax base with interest
and royalty payments. Next, our analysis lacks dynamics and we require proﬁts to be
repatriated to the home country. Thus, deferral is no option, and we miss out on the
parking function associated with traditional low-tax havens, as discussed by Mintz
and Weichenrieder (2010). Finally, the econometric analysis could go beyond cross-
sectional regressions. The impact of the centrality measure on FDI stocks could be
analysed in a panel setting with country-pair ﬁxed effects, although the time varia-
tion with respect to the tax parameters is limited. Another possibility would be to
investigate the relationship between FDI stocks and ﬂows in more depth using spatial
regression analysis as in Blonigen et al. (2007). Even without FDI diversion these
authors argue that FDI stocks in various regions could be interdependent. For FDI
stocks via conduit countries, the interdependence of stocks between various regions
could be even stronger.

Appendix A2: Tax havens
The ﬁrst three columns give the intersection of the Gravelle (2013) list of 50 tax havens
with our selection of 108 jurisdictions. The ﬁrst column presents the low-tax havens,
i.e. those with a corporate income tax rate in 2013 of 12.5% or less. Most of them
are islands. Malta could have been included in the list of low-tax havens because the
effective rate may be reduced to 10% or even less by a refund mechanism, although
it has a nominal rate of 35% (Loyens and Loeff 2013). We refrain from using such
characteristics and stick to the bare tax parameters (Table 14).
The second column indicates the ﬁve countries from the Gravelle list that we have
treated differently with respect to the CFC rules. We believe these ﬁve countries are
not subjected to the same anti-abuse scrutiny as are the jurisdictions in the ﬁrst and
third column. The Gravelle list is based on an overview of other papers classifying
tax havens. The ﬁrst four appear only in the list of Dharmapala and Hines (2009) and
Hines and Rice (1994), and Singapore is often considered as another ﬁnancial centre,
different from other tax havens (OECD “grey” list).
The third column contains the other tax havens in our analysis that are deprived of
preferential tax relief by those countries indicated to apply CFC rules. These are neither
low-tax havens nor ﬁnancial centres. The fourth column contains, for comparison, the
other countries in the country set with a low tax rate, i.e. a CIT of 12.5% or less.

the last preceding jurisdiction is credited, whether this CIT is paid or not. In the latter
case also the withholding tax would be credited.41
The option of crediting the nominal CIT of the last preceding country, option iii),
has the advantage that nothing needs to be known of the route before that last country
visited. Moreover, it ﬁts into the method described above, with the deﬁnition of tax
distance also given in the main text:

The ﬁrst option may be most in line with the philosophy of the credit method, i.e.
capital export neutrality. In practice it may be difﬁcult, or undesirable, to account for
all the accumulated taxes paid on a tax route. These total taxes include the treatment of
the CIT of the evaluated jurisdiction. However, it must be realized that the treatment of
the CIT in the jurisdiction under consideration is based on the initial distance matrix,
so that the credit is based on the nominal tax rate of the previous jurisdiction on the
path instead of the actual total taxes paid. This excludes implementing the ﬁrst two
options.
Acknowledging that the practice of the credit method is complex and that we have
no structural information on the actual operation of the method we decided on the next
implementation: we let the world average CIT be credited,42 in combination with the
actual withholding tax of the last conduit jurisdiction preceding the parent jurisdiction
with the credit method.
We believe this solution is the most realistic among possible alternatives. A ﬁrst
alternative approach would be to assume that no taxes at all were paid so that no
credits are applied. This would seriously underestimate the potential reduction of the
tax burden for MNEs by treaty shopping. We ﬁnd a world average optimal dividend
repatriation tax of 9.87% instead of 5.75%. On the other hand, taking the statutory
CIT of a conduit country as the basis for tax credit would overestimate the poten-
tial reduction as this CIT is not likely to be paid in full because of double tax relief.

Generating all shortest paths, and all those within range
The Floyd–Warshall algorithm is an efﬁcient method to compute the value of the
strict shortest paths for all pairs of nodes of a network. With a small addition to the
algorithm, the so-called penultimate vertex matrix (PVM) can be maintained. Upon
completion of the Floyd–Warshall algorithm, shortest paths for all country pairs can
be reconstructed from this matrix. The PVM method generates only a single strict
shortest path for a given pair. We, however, require all shortest paths of a given pair,
to be able to compute centrality measures. In addition, we are also interested in those
paths for a given pair with a length that is within a prespeciﬁed admissible range on
top of the value of the strictly shortest path. The PVM method is not suitable for
generating all those relevant paths.
Instead we implement a branch and bound method. The branching consists of a full,
depth-ﬁrst enumeration of all possible combinations. The bounding is accomplished
with the values of the strict shortest paths which were computed with the Floyd–War-
shall algorithm, executed beforehand. This implementation is a brute-force approach.
It is only possible because the relevant paths are not too long, with a sequence of ﬁve or
six countries as a maximum. Polak (2015) describes a relative efﬁcient implementation
of the brute-force method.
